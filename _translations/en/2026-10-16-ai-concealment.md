**Key Takeaway**

Across four experiments, people receiving AI assistance were judged more negatively than people receiving comparable help from colleagues or other tools. The penalty was weaker among evaluators experienced with AI. Stating explicitly that AI was useful for a task offset the suitability penalty, even though perceptions of laziness could remain. Technology has entered work faster than our norms for judging effort and competence have adapted.

### Why Employees Hide AI-Assisted Work

Reif, J. A., Larrick, R. P., & Soll, J. B. (2025). Evidence of a social evaluation penalty for using AI. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*(19), e2426766122.

The Duke University researchers separately examined users' expectations and observers' evaluations in four preregistered online experiments, totaling 4,439 participants. People anticipated appearing lazy and less competent, and observers did evaluate AI users in those terms.

The data came from online convenience samples in scenario-based experiments between March 2024 and February 2025. Whether the same patterns occur in actual workplace appraisal and task allocation remains an open empirical question.
</div>

## 01 · What the Four Experiments Found

| Study | Design | Main findings |
|---|---|---|
| Study 1, N = 497 | Imagine using either generative AI or a dashboard-creation tool to prepare a work report faster. | Those in the AI condition expected to seem lazier, more replaceable, less competent, and less diligent, and reported less willingness to disclose tool use. |
| Study 2, N = 1,203 | Randomly assigned employee profiles varied age, gender, occupation, and assistance source across 384 versions. Equivalent help came from AI or a person. | AI-assisted employees were judged lazier, less competent, less diligent, and less independent than employees receiving human help or no help. Effects did not depend on the employee's age, gender, or occupation. |
| Study 3, N = 1,668 managers | Incentivized hiring decisions between actual task performers described as daily AI users or nonusers. | Average suitability ratings did not differ (P = .516). Managers with little AI experience preferred nonusers; frequent AI users preferred AI users. Applicants' actual task performance was equivalent. |
| Study 4, N = 1,002 | Hire an AI-tool or MS Office user for either handwritten letters or an AI-suitable email task. | Perceived laziness provided a pathway to lower suitability ratings. Explicitly describing AI as useful for the email task offset the suitability penalty. |

The source of the assistance mattered. Evaluations differed when otherwise comparable help came from AI. The differences centered on effort and competence, rather than interpersonal competitive traits such as ambition or dominance.

## 02 · Concealment Can Be Individually Rational

Studies 1 and 2 together explain the logic: people anticipate a penalty, and observers impose one in their evaluations. Concealment can therefore serve an individual's interests. The authors describe this social evaluation penalty as an overlooked barrier to adoption. The obstacle is not only distrust of the technology, but how its users are seen.

Organizations can then face a paradox: people improving productivity with AI conceal the practice, leaving the organization unable to identify who is using AI effectively or how.

## 03 · When Does the Penalty Weaken?

**The evaluator's own AI experience matters.** Evaluators using AI at least weekly did not judge AI users as lazier in Study 4. In Study 3, managers' experience also shaped hiring preferences. The more negative judgments were concentrated among less experienced evaluators.

**The legitimate connection between task and tool matters.** Explicitly stating that generative AI was useful and permitted for the email task removed the suitability difference. A perception of greater laziness nevertheless remained. In the manual task, where AI was not useful, the suitability penalty persisted.

**Evidence boundary:** A supplementary study found that being told most colleagues use AI did not reduce the penalty. Diffusion alone was insufficient. Personal experience and norms legitimizing the use mattered. The moderation by evaluator experience was significant for Study 3's hiring choice; Study 4's hiring recommendation showed the same direction without reaching statistical significance (P = .103).

## 04 · Can This Be Understood as Cultural Lag?

> **DrSong's View · Cultural Lag**
>
> Ogburn's (1922) concept of cultural lag offers one interpretation: material culture, including technology, changes faster than the norms, values, and institutions expected to accommodate it. AI tools have entered workplaces whose evaluative norms may still equate visible individual effort with competence. People value effort itself as well as outcomes (Inzlicht et al., 2018); when AI reduces effort, the user's commitment and capability can become suspect. A weaker penalty among experienced AI evaluators is consistent with norms gradually adjusting to technological change.

> **DrSong's View · The Limits of That Explanation**
>
> Cultural lag alone is insufficient. It describes a societal gap, not the psychological mechanism behind an individual's judgment. Attribution theory helps address that level: observers may explain AI assistance in terms of a user's lack of capability or willingness rather than the situation (Weiner, 1986). Research on the social cost of seeking help also shows how assistance can be interpreted as dependence or incompetence (Lee, 2002). Nor should cultural lag imply that time will inevitably solve the problem. The supplementary study found that widespread use alone did not remove the penalty. Evaluators' experience and organizational standards require deliberate attention.

> **DrSong's View · Organizational Learning**
>
> Concealment's largest cost may be interrupted organizational learning. Good practices stay private, while poor practices escape scrutiny. Psychological safety allows people to reveal mistakes and requests for help without fearing interpersonal penalties (Edmondson, 1999). Yet concerns about AI are not all prejudice: as earlier Insights argue, AI can improve an output while bypassing learning. The better question is how AI was used. Sharing an initial draft, comparing it with AI's response, and explaining what was accepted, revised, or rejected need to be safe and recognized contributions. Then concealment has a chance to become shared learning.

## 05 · What HRD Can Do

- **Legitimize appropriate AI use at the task level.** State which work benefits from AI and how its use is recommended. Study 4 suggests this can offset suitability penalties under the conditions examined.
- **Give evaluators direct experience with AI.** Management training can help evaluators understand the work as well as the tool and may offer a way to address evaluation bias.
- **Move evaluation toward the quality of judgment.** Examine whether people verify and explain outputs, rather than treating AI use itself as the criterion.
- **Create ways to make the process visible.** Recognize shared examples, coaching, and knowledge transfer so that revealing sound AI practices has value.

### Insight Questions

1. In our organization, is disclosing AI use more advantageous than hiding it?
2. Do the people evaluating AI-assisted work have substantial firsthand experience with the tools?
3. Are we judging whether AI was used, or how it was used?
