**Key Takeaway**

Redesigning work changes how an organization operates; redesigning HR changes who benefits from that shift. When AI recommends people on the basis of past performance and experience, **opportunities may repeatedly go to those who already have experience, while others remain outside the pool.** Equity must therefore be a design principle rather than an audit after the fact. As AI enters appraisal and decision-making, organizations need to design how human contributions are recognized, who has authority to judge, how decisions can be reviewed, and where people can learn after automation.

## 01 · Work Got Faster. Who Gained the Chance to Grow?

Organizations also need to redesign the standards for the skills required to work with AI, for recognizing each person's contribution, and for deciding who can learn new work.

Consider an AI system recommending employees for a strategic project. It analyzes past performance and project records. People who have already handled important assignments and delivered results rise to the top; the manager can compare candidates more quickly, and the shortlist looks convincing. After several rounds, however, the same names may keep appearing. Employees with project experience are recommended again, while those without it remain outside the shortlist.

> **Dr. Yun Jeong Ro's View · Can the criteria for finding capable people also develop them?**<br>
> A recommendation can be reasonable under its stated criteria. But criteria that identify who can perform well now may be ill suited to helping others become ready in the future. Choosing a proven employee can be necessary for a project's success. If that is the only choice made repeatedly, when will anyone else have the chance to become proven?

## 02 · Equity Is a Design Principle, Not an Audit after the Fact

Past experience is valuable evidence. It reflects a person's abilities, but it also records that someone had access to an opportunity. When those meanings are conflated, “has not done this” can be read as “cannot do this,” and a lack of opportunity to gain experience becomes an apparent personal deficiency.

Trzebiatowski and colleagues (2026) propose *Equity by Design*, a theoretical framework for building equity into HR-AI systems. Starting from a strengths-based orientation and meaningful relational connections, they ask organizations to examine appraisal standards and rules for distributing opportunities. The point is to decide from the beginning what counts as evidence of performance and potential, rather than checking only for bias in the results afterward.

Skills-based HR, discussed by Kim Kwangtae and colleagues (2026), aims to identify the skills people actually possess and use that information in hiring, placement, development, and career planning alongside qualifications and job titles. If AI infers skills from existing work records, people with rich records may be well represented while unrecorded experience is missed. HR should therefore examine the evidence behind a skill inference as closely as the skills the system reports, and give employees a way to explain their experience and correct omissions.

> **Dr. Yun Jeong Ro's View · Do not mistake limited experience for limited ability**<br>
> Some companies now develop AI champions and offer hackathons and other projects to spread practical uses of AI. If selection gives heavy weight to previous project experience, limited access to those projects may be mistaken for limited ability. HR must design more than the recommendation: it must examine who enters the candidate pool, missing data, the job relevance of eligibility criteria, and the feedback and review process after a recommendation. Considering equity does not mean lowering standards. It means identifying the needed capabilities more accurately and opening paths for people to demonstrate them.

## 03 · AI Can Narrow or Widen Performance Gaps

An AI-assisted final product rarely reveals every human contribution. Someone may have framed the question carefully or caught an error in a plausible answer, yet those efforts may be invisible in the finished document. Established reputations can fill that gap: a well-regarded employee is praised for good work, while another may be told that AI did it.

Call and colleagues (2026) theoretically propose an AI-specific Matthew effect: generative AI may amplify the advantage of high performers when combined with their domain expertise, strategic use, and ability to organize work. AI helping a novice complete a task and AI magnifying an expert's advantage are distinct possibilities.

HR therefore needs to ask more than who uses AI most often. Who has the time and authority to try new things? Who can access good data and mentoring? Who receives credit for the work behind an AI-assisted result? Productivity measures should recognize problem framing, verification, judgment, collaboration, and knowledge sharing alongside output volume.

> **Dr. Yun Jeong Ro's View · Ask what improved the result**<br>
> In practice, questions can uncover the human work behind a result: the ability to define a problem, examine errors, and apply an answer to the organization's context.
>
> - What problem were you trying to solve?
> - Which parts of the AI answer did you accept or reject, and why?
> - How did you verify the result and adapt it to the situation?

## 04 · When AI Participates in Appraisal, What Happens to Human Judgment?

How much appraisal authority to give AI matters. In a mixed-method study, Pan and colleagues (2026) report that the AI appraiser's characteristics and the division of authority between people and AI affect employees' satisfaction with performance appraisal. The design of AI's role and the process matters more than its mere presence. Zheng and colleagues (2026) likewise find that giving AI final authority over HR decisions can harm an organization's reputation.

Simply declaring that a person makes the final decision does not complete a responsible process. Appraisers need to understand the basis for AI recommendations and have the authority and time to correct errors. Employees need to know the criteria behind decisions and how to request a review. HR must attend to procedural fairness and relational trust as well as technical accuracy.

## 05 · The Efficiency Paradox: Should Every Exception Disappear?

Yang (2026) theoretically proposes an *efficiency trap*: agentic AI may make HR execution more consistent while reducing the flexible interpretation and adjustment through which an organization adapts. In complex personnel matters, a manager's contextual judgment may be an organizational resource for responding to change and exceptions, rather than mere error or variation.

Organizations should distinguish decisions suited to automation from those requiring human discretion. In consequential HR decisions, exception handling, appeals, regular review of standards, and input from the people closest to the work should be part of the process. Standardization and adaptability are both outcomes to manage.

## 06 · HRD Must Renew Capabilities, Not Merely Use Them Up

If AI takes over routine entry-level work, the opportunities to build skill through that work may disappear too. HRD must decide how people will develop higher-order judgment after automation and what learning experiences can replace the tasks that have been removed. This means considering efficiency alongside learning, sustainable careers, and opportunities for vulnerable employees (Cooke et al., 2026).

SHRM's (2026) survey on AI in HR also emphasizes pairing AI use with human judgment and having HR leaders guide implementation and governance.

> **Dr. Yun Jeong Ro's View · Turning AI analysis into action on the ground**<br>
> Even if AI analyzes sales and staffing data for a store manager, the ability to understand employees' circumstances, set priorities, and coach a team remains essential. Training should go beyond learning how to operate AI. It should develop practical judgment for checking an analysis and turning it into action. AI can accelerate HR decisions, but the organization must still decide which contributions to recognize, which experiences become growth opportunities, and who is accountable for exceptions and errors.

If AI has changed how people work, organizations must also change how they evaluate, develop, and distribute opportunities to people.
