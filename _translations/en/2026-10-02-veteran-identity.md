**Key Takeaway**

AI may help almost anyone perform well. For someone whose hard-earned expertise has become central to their sense of self, that very development can be threatening. The most knowledgeable person may be the first to withdraw—not from laziness, but because something about who they are has been unsettled.
</div>

## AI Paradox 3

## 01 · The Most Respected Person Becomes the Quietest

Imagine a quality assurance manager with 20 years of experience, able to identify the source of a defect from a handful of reports. An AI-based defect analysis system arrives, and an employee in their second year reaches a similar conclusion with a few clicks.

The manager begins missing system training and speaks less in meetings. Executives describe this as resistance to change. The manager tells a different story: “I no longer know why this team needs me.”

## 02 · The Threat Experienced Employees Can Feel

Ibarra (1999) showed how professionals adapting to new roles experiment with **provisional selves**, trying different ways of acting and presenting themselves rather than simply planning a new identity in their heads. A new professional self takes shape through experience.

When people experience an identity threat, they may avoid it, deny it, or respond by changing their identity (Petriglieri, 2011). Their response depends in part on whether the environment allows them to try a new role safely.

## 03 · AI Unsettles the Equation “My Expertise Is Who I Am”

In a case study of a lending business introducing substitutive decision-making AI, employees lost opportunities to intervene in system decisions and confronted questions about their professional role identity. They tried in different ways to protect and reinforce that identity (Strich et al., 2021). When these efforts are blocked, people may avoid a new tool, fall further behind, and experience still greater threat.

Psychological safety—the sense that one can reveal mistakes without unacceptable interpersonal consequences—helps people attempt unfamiliar roles (Edmondson & Lei, 2014). Motivation is supported when they experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Simply telling an experienced employee to learn a new tool can unsettle all three.

## 04 · Give Experts Room to Try New Roles

**1. Discuss the role before assigning training.** Before introducing AI, meet experienced employees individually to distinguish what AI will do, what they can uniquely contribute, and what new responsibilities they might take on. Without this conversation, training may feel like notice that they are being replaced.

**2. Give experienced employees responsibility for reviewing AI outputs.** Ask them to review ambiguous cases and explain why the analysis is sound or mistaken. This shows through the work itself that expertise is moving into a role of correcting and guiding AI, rather than disappearing.

**3. Establish reciprocal mentoring pairs.** Pair a junior colleague familiar with the tool with an experienced practitioner. One shares how to use the technology; the other shares the standards behind workplace judgments. Two 30-minute sessions each month offer a practical starting point.

**4. Make a three-month trial role official.** Allow experienced employees to try being a learning designer, internal coach, or AI quality lead for three months before deciding whether to continue. Knowing that they can return to their previous role makes experimentation more feasible.

### Insight Questions

1. Which experienced employees have become quieter since AI was introduced?
2. Before telling them to learn, have we imagined a new role with them?
3. Do they have a genuine opportunity to try that role, including permission for the attempt not to work out?
