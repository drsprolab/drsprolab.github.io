### Who Pays for AI Adoption?

Shin, J., & Sucher, S. J. (2026, June 26). AI adoption is overloading your middle managers. *Harvard Business Review*.

The authors interviewed 18 partners, managers, and junior consultants across two large firms. They identify pressures involving learning, rewards, and differences between leaders' and managers' perceptions. A deeper concern is the leadership pipeline: when managers spend their time checking AI outputs, juniors may lose opportunities to learn judgment by working beside them. These are qualitative findings from consulting; generalization to other industries requires further evidence.
</div>

## 01 · Roles Rise at the Top and Bottom; Work Accumulates in the Middle

In teams adapting well, the authors describe **role elevation**. Juniors report completing research that once took days in thirty minutes and entering interpretation and strategy discussions earlier. Partners move from selling methodologies toward offering AI-enhanced judgment (Shin & Sucher, 2026).

Managers, however, may gain responsibilities without a corresponding redesign of their role. The article's composite account includes learning prompt techniques before work, answering clients' AI questions, checking outputs for errors, coaching recruits who have never built a report from scratch, and documenting what worked at the end of the day. This is a synthesis of interviews, not the documented day of one individual.

Much of the burden concerns **workslop**: plausible-looking AI material that lacks the substance needed to advance the work (Niederhoffer et al., 2025). Managers inherit responsibility for filtering it. The source's central argument is that managers need organizational support if AI is to elevate their contribution rather than bury them in additional work.

## 02 · Three Sources of Pressure

**1. Learning is informal; delivery deadlines are not.** Time saved by AI flows straight into client work. Effective prompts and workflows remain scattered among individuals, and teams repeatedly solve the same problems. Better-adapting teams temporarily reduce utilization targets, establish weekly sharing sessions, and create a searchable internal AI hub.

**2. Rewards recognize the wrong contributions.** Billable hours and individual outputs remain central, while sharing prompts and coaching colleagues receive little recognition. Some employees conceal AI use. The authors suggest acknowledging managers' combined responsibilities for experimentation, delivery, and development, and recognizing coaching and knowledge transfer in appraisal.

**3. Leaders and managers inhabit different realities.** Managers make local decisions about when an AI output is good enough and what juniors still need to learn firsthand. The proposed responses include leaders joining practical sessions, AI supervision training for managers—including fact-checking and detecting hallucinations—and clearer organization-wide standards.

## 03 · Figures That Put the Issue in Context

| Source | Reported pattern |
|---|---|
| McKinsey & Company (2025) | 88% of organizations use AI in at least one function. |
| BCG (2024) | 26% of companies have the capabilities to move beyond pilots and generate tangible value from AI. |
| Gartner (2024) | A forecast that, by 2026, one in five organizations would use AI to flatten structures and eliminate more than half of middle-management roles. This is a prediction, not an observed outcome. |
| Gallup (2026) | Manager engagement declined from 30% in 2023 to 22% in 2025, the largest decline among employee groups. |
| Lovich et al. (2025) | 76% of executives believed employees were enthusiastic about AI, compared with 31% of employees reporting that enthusiasm themselves. |

**Evidence boundary:** The source analysis rests on 18 semi-structured interviews in two consulting firms. Statements such as “days to thirty minutes” are participants' accounts, not directly measured productivity effects. The authors suggest that the patterns may be familiar elsewhere; they do not establish generalizability.

## 04 · What Might This Mean for Learning?

A new employee quickly produces a convincing AI-assisted report. A manager spends the day reviewing it. The junior has little opportunity to watch how that manager challenges an analysis or persuades a difficult client. Output accelerates while the channel through which judgment develops narrows—a problem for the leadership pipeline, not just the workflow.

> **DrSong's View · Learning**
>
> Practitioners develop into leaders through participation in communities of practice, not only through courses. Lave and Wenger (1991) describe legitimate peripheral participation: novices observe experienced practitioners, begin contributing, and gradually move toward fuller participation. When AI produces the output and managers are tied up checking it, juniors may complete deliverables without participating in the moments where judgment is exercised. The output remains, but the developmental path can be left empty.

> **DrSong's View · Organizational Learning**
>
> The source's first pressure point can be read as an organizational learning problem. In the 4I framework, individual insights must pass through interpretation and integration to become institutionalized (Crossan et al., 1999). Effective prompts and review techniques scattered among individuals suggest learning that has not traveled far enough. An internal AI hub and regular sharing sessions can help make that connection. Middle managers are among the people expected to sustain it.

### Insight Questions

1. Who is actually bearing the costs of AI adoption in our organization?
2. Is the time saved going to learning and coaching, or being absorbed by additional assignments?
3. Do our managers still have the capacity to develop the next generation of leaders?
