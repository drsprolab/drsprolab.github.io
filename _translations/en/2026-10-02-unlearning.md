**Key Takeaway**

AI learns from historical data and can confidently recommend what an organization has done well before—even after the environment changes. The tool that knows yesterday's success best may become an obstacle to tomorrow's adaptation.

## AI Paradox 5

## 01 · When the Past Recommends the Future

Imagine a retailer introducing an AI product-planning system trained on ten years of sales. Every season, it recommends variations on last year's bestsellers, often accurately. The planning team grows accustomed to following its suggestions.

Three years later, younger customers change their buying habits substantially, and the company responds slowly. It has little evidence from trying anything new. The system continues recommending past successes; people have fallen out of the habit of questioning it.

## 02 · Why Successful Organizations Miss Change

March (1991) described organizational learning through the tension between **exploration** and **exploitation**. Exploitation refines familiar approaches to deliver reliable performance; exploration investigates uncertain, new possibilities. Because exploitation produces more immediate and predictable returns, organizations can gradually favor it. When circumstances change, they may find themselves in a **success trap**, having lost the capacity to adapt.

## 03 · AI Can Tilt the Balance toward Exploitation

Raisch and Krakowski (2021) describe an **automation–augmentation paradox**. Automation, in which AI substitutes for human work, and augmentation, in which people and AI work together, are interdependent rather than wholly separate choices. Overemphasizing one can generate reinforcing cycles of undesirable outcomes. Replacing human decision-making with machine learning may reduce diversity in organizational practices and erode the contextual and causal understanding through which people learn, deepening learning myopia (Balasubramanian et al., 2022).

This makes **unlearning**—deliberately setting aside outdated knowledge and routines—particularly relevant (Klammer & Gueldenberg, 2019). What needs to be relinquished is not the data itself, but the assumption that a familiar approach will always be right.

## 04 · Re-examine Familiar Formulas for Success

**1. Require one counter-scenario alongside an AI recommendation.** In important planning meetings, include an alternative explaining what evidence might support taking the opposite direction. Rotate responsibility for developing it.

**2. Reserve 10% of time and budget for exploration.** Devote a portion of team goals to attempts AI has not recommended. Evaluate this allocation in terms of what was learned, rather than only immediate performance.

**3. Hold a quarterly meeting on practices to retire.** In one hour, identify three team principles that were reasonable three years ago but now merit questioning. Choose one to change experimentally next quarter.

**4. Invite an outside perspective regularly.** Twice a year, HRD can ask practitioners from another industry or newly hired employees to review core working practices and describe what seems unusual to them.

### Insight Questions

1. When did our team last seriously question an AI recommendation?
2. What share of our goals is genuinely reserved for exploration?
3. Which of our current formulas for success is most likely to become outdated first?
