**Key Takeaway**

AI often gives novices the greatest immediate performance boost. Expertise, however, develops through repeatedly tackling difficult tasks, making mistakes, and correcting them. When AI takes over that experience, an employee may perform impressively on the first day yet show little development three years later.
</div>

## AI Paradox 2

## 01 · Everyone Reaches the Same Plateau

Suppose a customer support center introduces an AI response recommendation tool. Within the first month, new agents' handling speed and customer satisfaction approach those of experienced colleagues. Management halves the onboarding period.

Two years later, a problem emerges. More complex complaints fall outside the recommended responses. Most second-year agents cannot decide how to proceed without suggested wording. Only a few colleagues who joined before the tool's introduction can independently judge how to handle exceptions.

## 02 · How Expertise Develops

Ericsson and colleagues (1993) argued that expertise grows through **deliberate practice**: repeatedly working on tasks slightly beyond one's current ability while receiving feedback. Later meta-analytic work showed that deliberate practice does not explain all differences in performance, although its contribution can be substantial and varies across domains (Macnamara et al., 2014).

Such practice requires **experiencing difficulty firsthand**. Becoming stuck, making an error, receiving feedback, and trying again help develop the standards by which people judge their work.

## 03 · When AI Removes Part of the Development Path

In a large study introducing a generative AI assistant to customer support work, productivity gains were greatest among less experienced agents (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). AI was, in effect, making experienced colleagues' know-how available to novices more quickly.

The important question is whether that know-how **becomes part of the learner's own capability**. In hospitals introducing robotic surgery, residents had few opportunities to practice operating through approved training routes. Some acquired skills through informal, unauthorized “shadow learning” instead (Beane, 2019). As technology takes over more of the work, formal opportunities to learn through difficulty may recede. A tool that improves today's performance can also leave an organization with fewer opportunities to develop tomorrow's experts.

## 04 · Turn Novice Experience into Expertise

**1. Identify core tasks that people must be able to perform without AI.** Work with practitioners to select three to five judgment-intensive tasks for each role. In customer support, these might include choosing an approach when no recommendation is available or calming a highly distressed customer. During their first six months, new employees could complete each task at least five times without AI, with feedback from an experienced colleague.

**2. Keep a three-part record: AI recommendation → my judgment → colleague feedback.** After a difficult case, record what AI recommended, what the employee actually did and why, and one line of feedback. Ten cases a month can provide a manageable starting point. Over time, these records become a personal learning portfolio.

**3. Build a ladder of difficulty rather than simply shortening training.** Use the time released by AI to organize progressively more demanding practical tasks. Reduce reliance on AI as learners move through the stages.

**4. Recognize experienced colleagues' explanations as part of their work.** Formally allocate two working hours a month for experts to explain their reasoning while handling difficult cases. This is an inexpensive way to turn shadow learning into legitimate learning opportunities.

### Insight Questions

1. Which tasks now handled by AI previously gave novices opportunities to learn?
2. What judgments should new employees be able to make without AI, and where can they practice them?
3. Where will our organization's independently capable experts come from three years from now?
