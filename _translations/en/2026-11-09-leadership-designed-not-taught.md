**Key Takeaway**

Leadership training is among the largest investments in corporate HRD, yet the same line item is funded again every year. Research has long shown that whether training content is used at work depends on organizational conditions such as learners' motivation, support from bosses and colleagues, and opportunities to apply it, and that leaders learn more from challenging assignments and their bosses than in a classroom. So the question should shift from "Which program should we open?" to **"How do we help people draw on their work experience, reflect on it, and turn it into learning?"** Training delivers the most when it is placed inside that flow, as a moment to organize experience.

### Why Leaders Do Not Change Even as Training Grows

Beer, M., Finnström, M., & Schrader, D. (2016). The great training robbery. *Harvard Business School Working Paper* No. 16-121, and five other sources.

This essay starts from the scale of corporate training investment in the United States (Training Magazine, 2025) and reads, in turn, the criticism that training does not turn into behavior change and results (Beer et al., 2016; Kaiser & Curphy, 2013), a meta-analysis of transfer of training (Blume et al., 2010) with its later review (Baldwin et al., 2017), and CCL's 70-20-10 guideline that leaders learn from experience.

The materials mix industry surveys, research papers, and a practitioner organization's guideline. No study directly compares experience-centered design with training in cost-effectiveness or selection accuracy. The proposal in the final section is this essay's own argument.
</div>

## 01 · The Investment That Is Largest and Never Ends

Leadership training is one of the largest investments in corporate HRD. In Training magazine's 2025 industry report, U.S. companies with 100 or more employees spent a total of $102.8 billion on training, an average of $874 per learner (Training Magazine, 2025). Management and supervisory training took 13% of the training budget, tied for first with mandatory compliance training. For 13 years running, the largest share of organizations has said it will put more resources into this area than the year before (30% of respondents). Multiplying the total by the share gives roughly $13 billion a year spent on manager training, though that is a rough estimate.

Despite the scale, the feeling on the ground is different. Because of leaders trained last year, we plan more training this year, and next year's budget goes into the same line. This essay therefore asks two things. Why does training never end, however much is spent? And is training really producing better leaders?

## 02 · What Training Cannot Solve

Michael Beer of Harvard and colleagues (2016) point out that U.S. companies spent $164.2 billion on training in 2012, yet most firms failed to turn learning into behavior change or financial results at the individual and organizational level. They called this "The Great Training Robbery." Their example is symbolic. One electronics business unit sent nearly all employees to a week-long leadership program. Participants found it impressive but did not believe organizational effectiveness, culture, or results had changed. In an organization with an unclear strategy and lingering top-down leadership, it was impossible to act the way the training taught.

An Ohio State leadership study shows something similar. Training changed frontline supervisors' attitudes, but a follow-up found most had returned to their pre-training beliefs. The effect lasted only where the boss practiced and believed in the new leadership style. Whether training survives at work depended less on the classroom than on the boss and the organizational environment.

Educational research points the same way. Blume et al. (2010) meta-analyzed 89 empirical studies and found that transfer, meaning use of training content on the job, is related to cognitive ability, conscientiousness, motivation, and a supportive work environment. Factors such as motivation and work environment were more strongly linked to transfer for open skills such as leadership development than for closed skills such as computer software. Beer et al. report that only about 10% of programs are effective, a figure they cite from other research. Kaiser and Curphy (2013) go further and diagnose the leadership development industry itself as having failed. Because training does not turn into results, organizations train again, and that is why the same line item is funded every year.

## 03 · Leadership Is an Accumulation of Experience and Reflection

Let us change the view. Leadership can be seen as partly innate traits, or as applying abilities and dispositions differently across situations. Either way, the essence of how it forms lies in learning and reflecting while working from the first days of a career, and in watching and reflecting on one's own leaders. Thoughts such as "I won't do it that way" or "I'd like to be like that" build up into a person's leadership.

The Center for Creative Leadership's (CCL) "Lessons of Experience" research, which has studied executives' learning paths for more than 30 years, supports this. The 70-20-10 rule that came out of it is a guideline that leaders learn 70% from challenging experiences and assignments, 20% from developmental relationships, and 10% from coursework and training. CCL says challenging assignments are the largest source of key learning experiences in managers' careers (Center for Creative Leadership, 2025). In its four-country research (the United States, China, India, and Singapore), the learning sources important in common were bosses and senior people, handling crises, expanded scope of work, lateral moves, and new assignments. These ratios should be read as a guide to direction, not a precise measurement.

The same experience does not give everyone the same result. Two people take on the same project, and one turns the experience into learning while the other lets it pass. By the time an organization meets a leadership candidate, years or decades of experience have already accumulated. Whether someone becomes a good leader or an ordinary one depends on how they drew on that experience, how much they reflected on it, and whether they turned it into learning.

Training is not meaningless. CCL explains that even though training is only 10% of the whole, well-designed training acts as an amplifier that clarifies and encourages the other 90% of learning. It is a real bonus when leader training is offered as a chance to organize accumulated experience and check one's own thinking.

**What to keep in mind · Nature of the evidence:** The training investment figures come from an industry survey, and the 13% share and the $13 billion annual figure are rough estimates. Beer et al. (2016) is a working paper, and the "10% of programs are effective" figure is one they cite from other research. The 70-20-10 rule is a guideline rather than a precise measurement, and some critics say its empirical support is weak. No study discussed here directly compares experience-centered design with training.

## 04 · Start by Accepting the Limits of Training

Training can give concepts, language, and a frame for organizing one's thinking. But judgment and trust come only from accumulated experience of failing, adjusting, and looking back. Trying to cover training's limits by adding more training only raises the cost. Beer et al. say training succeeds only when learners' goals align with the training's purpose, bosses and colleagues support the new behavior, and there are chances, time, and budget to apply it. All of these conditions belong to the organizational system. They therefore propose an "inverse logic" in which the organizational system is changed first and individual training comes after.

> **DrSong's View · An Organization That Designs Leader Growth**
>
> The question organizations ask should change from "Which program should we open?" to "How do we help people draw on their work experience, reflect on it, and turn it into learning?" This includes designing challenging assignments early in a career, making reflection a daily habit through bosses' feedback and coaching, and building ways to record and share experience. Training delivers the most value when placed within this flow as a moment to organize experience.
>
> Leader selection changes too. Rather than training completed or scores, the basis for selection would be how someone has turned accumulated experience into learning over a career. That this approach would cost less and select more accurately than making leaders through training is this essay's argument, and no study directly comparing the two has been presented. Leaders are not made in a classroom. They grow inside the system called work, and the organization's role is to design and choose that growth.

---

### Insight Questions

1. For the behavior our leadership training aims to change, do **bosses and colleagues actually support it, and are there chances and time to apply it**?
2. Can we see **which challenging assignments our leader candidates have taken on and how they reflected on them to turn them into learning**?
3. Is our training designed as **a moment to organize and check experience**, or has training itself become the goal?
