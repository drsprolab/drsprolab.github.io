**Key Takeaway**

## AI Paradox 7

## 01 · When Training and Appraisal Send Different Messages

Imagine a financial services company offering an AI-driven work innovation course to every employee. The instructor encourages people to change how they work without fearing failure. Satisfaction is high, and ideas flow.

Yet that year's appraisal criteria remain unchanged: error counts, compliance, and adherence to schedules. An employee whose AI experiment causes a processing delay receives a poor evaluation. The following year, idea submissions to the innovation course fall by half.

## 02 · What Appraisals and Rewards Teach

Kerr (1975) observed that when organizations hope for one behavior but reward another, people follow the rewards. If an organization calls for innovation while rewarding flawless compliance, compliance is what it will obtain.

Schein and Schein (2017) locate **basic assumptions** beneath organizational culture. Unlike espoused values, these assumptions are revealed in what leaders attend to and reward. Employees learn them through daily experience, often more powerfully than they learn the message delivered in training.

## 03 · AI Can Amplify the Double Message

Transfer of training depends on conditions at work, including supervisory support and opportunities to apply learning, as well as the training itself (Blume et al., 2010). Early attempts to use AI inevitably involve trial and error. If that experimentation brings appraisal penalties, employees have reason to leave their learning unused and keep quiet.

People withhold improvement suggestions when speaking seems futile or risky (Morrison, 2014). Psychological safety, including the ability to acknowledge mistakes, is associated with learning behaviors and performance (Frazier et al., 2017). Expanding AI innovation training while leaving appraisal unchanged can reinforce the impression that what the organization says and what it actually values are different things.

## 04 · Bring Training and Evaluation into Alignment

**1. Review the appraisal form before designing the course.** HRD and HR should examine whether the behaviors a course encourages will disadvantage employees in their evaluation. If so, adjusting the evaluation conditions should come first.

**2. Establish a protected space for registered experiments.** Allow each team to register one or two AI work experiments per quarter and exclude agreed experimental delays or errors from appraisal penalties. A short record can state what will be tried, by when, and what the team expects to learn if it fails.

**3. Evaluate attempts and shared learning.** Add a criterion asking whether employees tried a new approach and shared the outcome. Look at the attempt and the sharing, not only whether the outcome succeeded.

**4. Ask managers to lead a failure-sharing conversation.** Once a month, set aside fifteen minutes. The manager describes an unsuccessful attempt first; team members follow. What leaders give their attention to helps shape culture.

### Insight Questions

1. Do the behaviors encouraged by our training match those rewarded by appraisal?
2. How are mistakes made while trying a new AI-supported approach treated in evaluation?
3. What would employees say they actually learned after an innovation course?
