**Key Takeaway**

In KReC, knowledge revision involves old and new information becoming active together, being integrated, and a better-connected new explanation gaining an advantage in subsequent activation. Recent AI studies show why assisted performance, independent learning, and accurate self-assessment should be examined separately. They also raise concerns about overly agreeable AI. None directly demonstrates that KReC's processes have weakened. The practical proposal is to help learners compare existing ideas with new information and check their understanding. Connections to organizational innovation remain to be tested.

### AI and the Conditions for Knowledge Revision

Kendeou, P. (2024). A theory of knowledge revision: The development of the KReC framework. *Educational Psychology Review, 36*(2), Article 44, read alongside six studies published in 2024–2026.

The question is whether knowledge is being revised while people work with AI. The evidence spans experiments and a survey of knowledge workers, with many studies involving students or online participants completing short tasks. None directly measured KReC-based knowledge revision. Using the framework to connect their findings is the interpretation developed in this essay.
</div>

## 01 · How KReC Explains Knowledge Revision

Song and Chermack (2008) connect individual learning with organizational knowledge formation, emphasizing the accumulation of knowledge and its embedding in culture. Here, we examine that process's individual starting point: correcting previously held misconceptions. Kendeou's (2024) account of KReC describes five principles for knowledge revision during reading and reviews empirical work comprising 55 experiments and 4,859 participants (Table 2, p. 9).

| Role | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption | Encoding | Information encoded in long-term memory is not simply erased or replaced; it can become active again. |
| Assumption | Passive activation | Relevant information can become active without deliberate retrieval, whether it helps or interferes with comprehension. |
| Condition 1 | Co-activation | The earlier misconception and corrective information need to be active together for the discrepancy to become available. |
| Condition 2 | Integration | The conflicting information is linked within a common memory network. |
| Condition 3 | Competing activation | A better-connected corrective explanation draws activation away from the misconception, reducing its later interference. |

KReC operationally defines revision through the elimination of measurable activation of previously held incorrect knowledge (Kendeou, 2024, p. 10). It does not imply that the old memory trace has been deleted. The issue is whether richer connections around the correction reduce reactivation and interference. **Competing activation is a mechanism of memory activation, not the same thing as consciously debating an argument.** Saying that refutation brings ideas together while explanation helps one prevail is a metaphor for understanding it.

## 02 · Recent Research on AI and Cognition

| Study | Participants and design | Main findings |
|---|---|---|
| Lee et al. (2025), CHI | Self-report survey: 319 knowledge workers, 936 work examples. | Greater trust in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking; greater confidence in one's own task capability was associated with more. This is correlational. Verification, response integration, and task stewardship emerged as key activities. |
| Fan et al. (2025), BJET | 117 university students, randomly assigned to ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing analysis tool, or no support. | ChatGPT produced the largest improvement in essay scores without significantly greater knowledge acquisition or transfer than the other conditions. The authors interpret this as a risk of metacognitive laziness. |
| Bastani et al. (2025), PNAS | Classroom-randomized field experiment with approximately 1,000 students in one Turkish high school. | GPT Base raised practice scores by 48% but reduced unassisted test scores by 17%, relative to controls. GPT Tutor largely mitigated this negative effect using a combination of hints and teacher-provided solutions, common errors, and feedback. Its unassisted test performance was not significantly above the control group. |
| Fernandes et al. (2026), CHB | Study 1: 246 participants; replication: 452; 20 LSAT reasoning items. | Study 1's AI users outperformed a historical non-AI benchmark by about three points but overestimated their AI-assisted score by about four. AI literacy had a small negative association with metacognitive accuracy. The authors' modeling did not show the usual Dunning–Kruger pattern among AI users on this task. |
| Cheng et al. (2026), Science | Analysis of 11 leading AI models and three preregistered experiments, N = 2,405. | On the personal-advice questions examined, AI affirmed users' behavior 49% more often than human respondents on average. One interaction with sycophantic AI reduced willingness to take responsibility and repair interpersonal conflict while strengthening conviction of being right. Participants nevertheless preferred and trusted that AI more. |
| Doshi & Hauser (2024), Science Advances | Randomized online experiment: 293 story writers, 600 evaluators. | Access to AI ideas improved average ratings of short stories' creativity and writing quality, while stories became more similar to one another. The study did not measure lasting creative capability or organizational innovation. |

**Evidence boundaries:** Assisted outputs improved in several studies, without necessarily improving unassisted test performance or self-assessment. GPT Tutor combined multiple design elements; its outcome cannot be attributed to hints alone. Lee et al. measured self-reported associations, not causal changes in objectively measured thinking. Most tasks were short, so long-term effects remain uncertain. KReC itself primarily addresses factual misconceptions during reading, often among young adults; extending it to organizational beliefs and values requires care (Kendeou, 2024).

## 03 · Three Learning-Design Questions through a KReC Lens

> **DrSong's View · Knowledge Revision**
>
> These studies did not directly measure whether co-activation, integration, or competing activation had been weakened or bypassed. The following are design questions and hypotheses requiring further testing.
>
> **Co-activation: Are discrepancies between prior ideas and new evidence being examined?** Existing knowledge can become passively active while reading an AI answer. Writing one's own answer first is not a necessary step in KReC. However, the findings about trust, reported critical thinking, and sycophancy suggest examining whether users consider contrasting evidence. Recording an initial view is an educational proposal to support that comparison; its effect on co-activation needs separate evaluation.
>
> **Integration: Does better output become understanding and independent capability?** Fan et al.'s result means no significantly greater acquisition or transfer than comparison groups, not an absence of learning or integration. Bastani et al. found poorer unassisted performance in GPT Base, without directly measuring failed memory integration as its cause. These findings suggest assessing understanding separately through explanation or related unassisted tasks.
>
> **Competing activation: Is the corrected explanation accessible later?** KReC proposes that increasing the amount and interconnectedness of corrective information can reduce misconception reactivation and interference. This is a memory-level mechanism requiring appropriate measures. Limited critical review does not establish an absence of competing activation. Fernandes et al.'s overestimation finding measures self-assessment, not retrieval competition or knowledge durability. Whether evidence-linked explanations improve later accurate retrieval should be tested through delayed and transfer assessments.

The practical aim is to identify errors and connect accurate corrective information with explanatory support. This does not mean exposure to misinformation is harmless. In examining AI answers, learners need to ask not only what differs from their existing view, but which account is supported by trustworthy evidence.

## 04 · Why This Also Raises Questions about Innovation

> **DrSong's View · Organizational Knowledge and Innovation**
>
> If individual learning contributes to organizational knowledge formation (Song & Chermack, 2008), what people understand while using AI matters beyond the individual. Doshi and Hauser's (2024) stories received better average creativity ratings but were more similar to each other. This does not establish reduced organizational idea diversity or innovation. It suggests examining quality and diversity together. The earlier proposal of a Generative Cognitive Pause can be considered as a design experiment: what changes if team members develop initial ideas before using AI? It is neither a necessary KReC procedure nor a validated innovation intervention.

## 05 · Implications for HRD

These practices are proposals informed by the evidence. Their effects on KReC processes and organizational outcomes need evaluation in context.

- **Support comparison between prior thinking and new information.** Ask for a brief initial hypothesis and reasoning, then compare them with AI's answer. This activity was not tested by Lee et al. and is not a required condition of co-activation.
- **Seek contrary evidence and check its sources.** Asking AI for weaknesses and counterarguments may be useful, but AI-generated criticism also needs verification against original sources. A prompt alone cannot guarantee that sycophancy disappears.
- **Ask why a suggestion was accepted or revised.** Let people explain changes in their own words. A written record can support connections, but is not itself proof of memory integration.
- **Provide accurate corrections after error review.** After learners inspect an AI output containing errors, supply a verified explanation and evidence. Assess error detection and explanation on new tasks and delayed tests; the cited studies do not establish this exercise's effectiveness.
- **Combine hints with verified solutions and feedback in learning tools.** Evaluate learning without AI, and treat the GPT Tutor findings as evidence about the combined design rather than one isolated element.
- **Examine team idea quality and diversity together.** Try independently generated initial proposals before AI use, and compare the results. Whether the story-writing similarity effect appears in real teams and innovation outcomes remains to be established.

### Insight Questions

1. Do our employees put their own thinking into words before consulting AI?
2. Do we recognize the people who identify AI errors?
3. Alongside output quality, do we assess understanding that people can explain and apply without AI?
