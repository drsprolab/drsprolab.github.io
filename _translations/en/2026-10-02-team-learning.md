**Key Takeaway**

Meetings move faster when AI summarizes the material and drafts a conclusion. Teams learn, however, when information known only to individual members comes to light and changes how others think. A rapid consensus can sometimes be a warning that this has not happened.

## AI Paradox 6

## 01 · A Perfect Meeting in Twenty Minutes

Suppose a team is choosing a new supplier. The manager displays an AI-generated comparison, and everyone agrees on the top-ranked company. The meeting ends within twenty minutes.

Two months later, delivery problems emerge. The purchasing specialist had known about that supplier's earlier delays; the operations representative had heard about staffing shortages at its factory. Neither spoke up. Their information was absent from the summary, and agreement already seemed settled.

## 02 · The Information Meetings Tend to Miss

Stasser and Titus (1985) showed that group discussions tend to repeat information already shared by everyone while neglecting information held by just one member. When the information needed for a good decision is distributed across participants—a **hidden profile**—groups often reach the wrong conclusion.

A meta-analysis covering 25 years of research found that groups rarely solved hidden profiles successfully and that bringing unshared information into the discussion was critical to decision quality (Lu et al., 2012). For a group to outperform its individual members, it needs procedures for surfacing information as well as respect for different views (Schulz-Hardt & Mojzisch, 2012).

## 03 · AI Can Give Shared Information Even Greater Weight

AI summaries organize documented information: material available for everyone to see. A purchasing specialist's recollection or an operations colleague's local knowledge may never enter the table. When a polished summary frames the meeting, common information gains authority and dispersed information can become harder to introduce.

Speaking against an emerging consensus also takes courage. Where **psychological safety** is low, people are less willing to raise uncomfortable information (Frazier et al., 2017). Faster meetings can leave still less time to examine one another's thinking.

## 04 · Bring Different Information and Perspectives into the Meeting

**1. Write for three minutes before showing the AI summary.** Before an important decision, ask everyone to note information they may be the only person to know. Go around the room and invite each person to share one point.

**2. Assign someone to challenge the emerging conclusion.** Rotate responsibility for considering why a conclusion might be wrong. Making dissent a role turns it from a personal objection into a legitimate part of the process.

**3. Add a “what changed?” field to the minutes.** Record whether anyone's judgment changed during the meeting, and why. A persistently empty field is a reason to ask whether the meetings are creating learning.

**4. Teach managers a different order of speaking.** Practice asking, “Who has information we have not heard yet?” before offering an opinion. Managers speak last. HRD can rehearse this sequence through role-play.

### Insight Questions

1. Is our AI summary the starting point of discussion, or already its conclusion?
2. Has a meeting changed anyone's mind in the past month?
3. Do we have a procedure for bringing individually held information into the conversation?
