**Key Takeaway**

AI can be welcomed as the future while its users are judged less capable and the time needed to learn it goes unrecognized. Tools change, but evaluation and learning arrangements remain in place. Cultural lag will not necessarily resolve itself: concealed practices remain unshared, and those with fewer learning opportunities can fall further behind. Making AI a partner requires a culture that supports learning and recognizes appropriate use.

### When Technology Outpaces Culture

Lou et al. (2026), *Cultural lag, consumer literacy, and coping in AI-enabled advertising*, read alongside recent workplace and HRD research.

Lou and colleagues conducted twelve focus groups with 105 Singapore citizens. They found that beliefs, literacy, policy, and education were not keeping pace with AI-enabled advertising, and that participants relied largely on familiar coping strategies. This qualitative study concerns advertising and consumers. Its framework is borrowed here as a lens; evidence about work comes from the workplace studies discussed separately.
</div>

## 01 · Technology Arrives before Culture Catches Up

Ogburn described cultural lag as a weakening of adjustment between related parts of culture when one changes faster or more extensively than the other (Ogburn, 1957, as cited in Lou et al., 2026). Technology can move quickly while values, norms, and institutions change more slowly.

Many participants in Lou et al.'s study struggled to identify AI-generated advertising. They continued to cope through familiar practices such as checking sources, blocking advertisements, or distrusting advertising generally. An older participant's concern captured the difficulty: rapid change creates a persistent feeling of being behind.

## 02 · Workplace Cultural Lag: AI Use without Recognition

At work, the gap can appear as a failure to recognize AI use as a legitimate part of the job.

- **Evaluation.** AI-assisted employees were judged lazier and less competent than employees receiving comparable human help or no assistance (Reif et al., 2025). An older equation between personal effort and competence can remain influential.
- **Learning.** In interviews with seventeen HR professionals, barriers included insufficient time, direction, and recognition rather than simply a lack of willingness. Learning AI was easily postponed when it was absent from formal work responsibilities (Arístegui Monge et al., 2026).
- **Institutional arrangements.** A study of 18,000 Danish workers in eleven AI-exposed occupations found substantial perceived productivity potential, alongside constraints from employer restrictions and a perceived need for more training (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025).

Parry et al. (2021) use **organizational cultural lag** to describe organizations publicly supporting change while failing to establish consistent internal arrangements. Their original context is marriage equality in Australian sport. The concept also offers a way to interpret organizations celebrating AI transformation while devaluing its users.

## 03 · Why the Gap Matters

**AI practices become hidden.** Lack of recognition does not necessarily stop use. Dittmar and Sposato (2026) argue that employees develop practices through informal networks before formal training catches up. Such knowledge can vary in quality, create unintended privacy risks, and remain confined to the networks in which it arose.

**Inequalities may widen.** Without support, learning depends on individual time and interest. In the Danish study, women's reported workplace ChatGPT use was sixteen percentage points below men's (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025). Lou et al. similarly discuss how limited consumer AI literacy can deepen power imbalances. Time alone need not narrow these gaps.

**Training alone does not ensure changed practice.** In an eight-week study involving six hospital training professionals, instructional guides and microlearning videos increased knowledge without immediately changing practice. Confidence declined as participants learned more, which the researchers suggest may reflect more discriminating judgment rather than simple failure. They identify tool access, protected learning time, and institutional endorsement as important conditions (Naffi et al., 2026).

**Evidence boundaries:** The HRD sources include small qualitative studies with seventeen and six participants, and conceptual argument without empirical data in Dittmar and Sposato. They indicate issues and possible directions rather than effect magnitudes. This essay uses only the abstract-reported findings for Naffi et al.

## 04 · Partnership Requires a Culture That Can Sustain It

> **DrSong's View · Organizational Culture**
>
> Calling AI a partner does not establish a partnership. If using it damages an appraisal, learning it is not recognized as work, and its permitted boundaries remain unclear, AI stays a tool used in secret. Cultural lag can sound as though culture will inevitably catch up. Organizations cannot assume that. Lou et al.'s participants called for disclosure, consent over data use, and ethical standards: people wanted a say in how technology was used. Organizations should ask not only how quickly employees adapt, but what they have agreed about appropriate use.
>
> Responding to new technology with old practices can also be read through the knowledge revision issues discussed in the [earlier Insight](/weekly-insight/2026-10-18-knowledge-revision-ai/). New approaches need opportunities to be tried repeatedly and have their results recognized. Creating those conditions is part of HRD's work.

## 05 · What HRD Can Change

| Area | Practical action | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation | Ask whether employees can verify and explain outputs; record the scope of AI use and verification in one or two lines. | Reif et al. (2025) |
| Use standards | Provide concise guidance on suitable tasks, prohibited inputs, and disclosure. Explicit task suitability removed a suitability penalty in the experiment, within the conditions studied. | Reif et al.; Humlum & Vestergaard |
| Learning time | Allocate work time to AI learning and experimentation, and recognize it in development and performance conversations. | Arístegui Monge et al. |
| Hidden practices | Anonymously ask how many unapproved AI tools employees regularly use. Dittmar and Sposato propose that over a third reporting two or more signals widespread informal use; this is their diagnostic suggestion, not a validated cutoff. | Dittmar & Sposato |
| Practice into formal learning | Gather workplace techniques and verification approaches into a library and courses. The same authors suggest that a delay exceeding six months can signal a disconnect between training and practice. | Dittmar & Sposato |
| Application after training | Connect course participation with tool access, real work, and peer feedback. | Naffi et al. |
| Unequal access | Monitor use and learning participation across functions, grades, gender, and age. | Humlum & Vestergaard |

### Insight Questions

1. Is AI use recognized in our organization, or hidden?
2. Do we leave learning to individual motivation, or provide time, direction, and recognition?
3. What have we actually agreed about how AI should be used?
