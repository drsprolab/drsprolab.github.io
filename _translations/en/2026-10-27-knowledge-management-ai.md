**Key Takeaway**

Knowledge management is more than storage. It is the systemic and interpersonal practice connecting individual knowledge, shared beliefs, mental models, reflection, and renewed learning. In AI-supported cloud collaboration, notes can move straight into shared systems while the learning among people becomes harder to observe. The question is where organizational learning occurs—and how HRD can recognize and support it.

### Connecting Knowledge Management, Organizational Learning, and the Learning Organization

Lim, Song, and Yoon (2014) outline three generations of knowledge management and conditions for integration with organizational learning. Song and Chermack (2008) examine individual learning and organizational knowledge formation. Edgar and Albright (2023) analyze conceptual problems in knowledge management activities; Edwards and Lönnqvist (2023) identify emerging issues, including AI and false knowledge.

All four are conceptual or theoretical contributions, not empirical tests of organizational learning in generative AI environments. The fourth and “4.5” generations and the idea of less visible organizational learning are extensions proposed in this essay.
</div>

## 01 · From Three Generations to a Fourth—and a “4.5”

Lim, Song, and Yoon (2014) distinguish a first generation centered on technical knowledge and large databases, a second emphasizing knowledge workers and knowing where expertise resides, and a third enabling networked knowledge creation across time and space. The changes accompanying generative AI suggest two further distinctions.

{% include english-figure.html index=0 %}

In the proposed fourth generation, AI transforms human knowledge into information that appears knowledge-like. In “4.5,” people validate those outputs, establish and share knowledge, and make the verified material available as a future AI source. Knowledge is seen successively as a stored asset, a contextual flow, and a candidate requiring verification. Evolution of the system is not, however, the same as changed understanding and practice among its human members.

## 02 · Knowledge Management Connects Individual Knowledge with a Learning Organization

Song and Chermack (2008) describe organizational knowledge formation through reflective socialization, collaborative externalization, systematic storage, and cultural internalization. Internalized knowledge can become a **mental model** people take for granted. This enables shared standards, but can also harden into unexamined habit. **Double-loop learning** involves reflecting on outcomes and revising the assumptions and standards behind behavior, not merely adjusting the behavior itself. A culture capable of repeatedly renewing its knowledge is one way to understand a learning organization.

Knowledge management connects these processes through systems and human interaction. Storage and distribution help knowledge survive beyond particular individuals; application and revision make established knowledge available for learning again. Edgar and Albright's (2023) treatment of learning as a knowledge-management activity associated with a changed mental state can be read in this way. So can Lim, Song, and Yoon's (2014) emphasis on learning organization culture, structure, leadership, and technological systems as conditions for integration.

## 03 · When Organizational Learning Becomes Less Visible

Generative AI, GitHub, and shared “Second Brain” environments alter the path. Notes and commits enter a common repository immediately; AI retrieves and recombines them to answer another person's question. Individual contributions become part of an organizational information system without necessarily passing through shared interpretation, dialogue, and negotiation.

It would be premature to conclude that learning among people has disappeared. Its location and visibility may have changed. Which note is retrieved, which draft is merged, and which answer becomes the default can be shaped by algorithms. What observers see may be logs and version histories rather than the conversations, disagreements, and negotiations emphasized in classical accounts.

**Evidence boundary:** This diagnosis is an interpretation. None of the four sources directly studies these changes in generative AI collaboration spaces. Edwards and Lönnqvist (2023) identify AI and false knowledge as emerging issues and discuss earlier arguments that AI without knowledge management wastes effort and resources. Lim, Song, and Yoon (2014) also note the scarcity of empirical studies integrating knowledge management, organizational learning, and learning organizations.

> **DrSong's View · Organizational Learning**
>
> Under Song and Chermack's account, stored information that has not passed through human interpretation and negotiation is not automatically a shared belief. I see important learning opportunities in verification and challenge: asking for evidence during review, correcting or rejecting an AI answer, and recording why. Human-in-the-loop review should therefore be designed as a place for organizational learning. We should also watch for defaults hardening into mental models without anyone explicitly agreeing to them. Algorithms may optimize answers under a criterion without asking whether the criterion remains appropriate. Double-loop learning still requires people to pause and question it. The individual judgment discussed in the [earlier essay on learning](/weekly-insight/2026-10-22-redefining-learning-ai/) becomes collective verification and renewed questioning at the organizational level.

## 04 · How Can We Detect and Support What Is Harder to See?

If a process is less visible, we need to examine its traces. HRD can identify records that may signal learning among people and design procedures that make those records meaningful.

- **Detect revisions and rejections.** A near-zero rate of human changes to AI drafts is a reason to investigate the depth of review, rather than conclusive proof that no verification occurs.
- **Look for disagreement and reasons for revision.** Do review comments and commits explain why a change was made? Version history and reasoning help people revisit established knowledge (Edwards & Lönnqvist, 2023).
- **Make human approval substantive.** Record who approved what, on what evidence, and within which limits. An unsigned draft remains plausible information rather than an accountable shared judgment.
- **Create occasions to question the question.** Add that question to retrospectives. Give default answers and AI source material review dates so that accepted knowledge is examined again.

### Insight Questions

1. How much material in our shared repositories has undergone human review and agreement?
2. Which records can help us identify learning occurring within algorithm-mediated workflows?
3. Where do we revisit answers and criteria that have become defaults?
