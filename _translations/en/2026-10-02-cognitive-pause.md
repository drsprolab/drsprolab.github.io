**Key Takeaway**

AI is intended to help us think. Yet one of the first things it can displace is time spent attempting a problem for ourselves. The faster an answer arrives, the less opportunity we may have to make it our own.
</div>

## AI Paradox 9

## 01 · Five Uncomfortable Minutes

Picture a data analysis course for new employees. As soon as the task appears, learners open an AI chat window. Within minutes, they have code and an interpretation. Give them similar data the next day, however, and most start again by asking AI.

Now imagine changing one rule in the next course: **spend the first five minutes attempting the problem in your own way, without AI; then use the tool**. Participants find it frustrating. In this illustrative scenario, however, more of them can begin a similar problem independently the following day.

## 02 · What a First Attempt Leaves Behind

Information people generate for themselves is generally remembered better than information they merely read. Slamecka and Graf (1978) systematically demonstrated this **generation effect**: attempting to produce an answer contributes to its retention.

Subsequent work extends the idea. Struggling with a problem before receiving instruction can support conceptual understanding and transfer even when the initial attempt fails (Kapur, 2016). Prequestioning can improve later learning as well (Pan & Carpenter, 2023). The ICAP framework similarly connects more constructive and interactive forms of cognitive engagement with stronger learning outcomes (Chi & Wylie, 2014).

## 03 · AI Can Displace the Chance to Think First

In a randomized experiment with 1,625 students, learners writing peer feedback with AI guidance tended to rely on it rather than learn from it. After the guidance was withdrawn, a self-monitoring checklist did not fill the gap to the same extent as the AI support had (Darvishi et al., 2024). AI can take over the very first uncertain attempt. **When help arrives too quickly, there may be less room for learning to begin.**

## 04 · Preserve Thinking Time in Learning and Work

**1. Build five minutes of independent thinking into practical tasks.** Put the instruction at the top of the task sheet. A first attempt recorded on paper can later be compared with AI's response.

**2. Ask for a three-column comparison.** Have learners submit their initial approach, AI's suggestion, and the differences between them—including why they chose the approach they ultimately used.

**3. Send three questions before the course.** Email the next day's problems in advance and invite learners to think about them. They need not arrive with correct answers; the aim is a first attempt at thinking.

**4. Try “human first draft, AI refinement” at work.** Agree with managers that new employees and people moving into unfamiliar roles will initially outline their reports or analysis plans themselves, then refine them with AI. Follow their ability to begin independently over a three-month trial.

### Insight Questions

1. How many minutes in our courses are reserved for learners' first attempts without AI?
2. Do we collect only finished outputs, or also examine how a person's initial approach differed from AI's answer?
3. Could the message “start straight away with AI” be taking away opportunities to learn?
