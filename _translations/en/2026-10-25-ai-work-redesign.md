**Key Takeaway**

AI does not always leave people with spare time. Someone who has long wanted to improve a result may use saved time to raise quality or test a postponed idea; working hours may remain unchanged or even expand. This is not a justification for unlimited work. Organizations need to translate those ambitions into agreed goals, responsibilities, processes, and resources. **Work redesign** concerns the role and its responsibilities; **workflow redesign** concerns the sequence through which a particular outcome is produced.
</div>

### Why Might Skilled AI Users Take on More Work?

Brynjolfsson et al. (2025), *Generative AI at Work*, read alongside four other studies.

The argument is not that AI universally makes people work more. It is that people with clear goals and longstanding questions about improving their work may find previously impractical improvements within reach. Field and experimental studies identify conditions affecting speed and quality; job-crafting research examines proactive changes to work. No study cited here directly tests the full proposition that strongly improvement-oriented AI users work longer and improve organizational outcomes. That connection is offered as interpretation, not established fact.
</div>

## 01 · How AI Can Expand What People Attempt

A faster first draft can release time to compare sources, check missing evidence, or revisit alternatives previously abandoned. AI may expand the feasible scope of work as well as lower the cost of existing tasks. People with a clear sense of what needs improvement can direct that capacity toward problems they have held in mind for some time.

Effects differ across people and tasks. Among 5,172 customer support agents, access to generative AI increased issues resolved per hour by an average of 15%, with larger improvements among less experienced and less skilled agents. Highly experienced agents gained less in speed, and some conversation-quality measures declined (Brynjolfsson, Li, & Raymond, 2025). Speed and quality do not improve uniformly for everyone.

Hou et al. (2025) studied designers generating and implementing ideas with AI in two experiments. Novices and experts benefited creatively during ideation, but experts did not show the same benefit during implementation. In the laboratory task, experts spent 57% more time reaching creativity comparable to peers working without AI. The field experiment also identified implementation inefficiencies among experts. More time does not necessarily produce a better result; the interaction between the workflow and expertise matters.

There is evidence in the other direction as well. A six-month field experiment with 7,137 knowledge workers across 66 firms found that access to AI reduced email time and work outside regular hours, without evidence of a change in the amount or composition of assigned tasks (Dillon et al., forthcoming). Saving time does not itself determine whether the time goes to more valuable work. Work goals and processes also need attention.

## 02 · Who Is Well Positioned to Improve Work with AI?

The person of interest here is not simply someone willing to work long hours. It is someone who understands the purpose of their role and has concrete standards and questions about improving it:

- **Clear goals:** They know what the task should achieve and for whom.
- **Standards for quality:** They aim beyond submission toward accuracy, usefulness, or persuasive reasoning.
- **Questions about improvement:** They have postponed analyses, hypotheses, customer experience changes, or better ways of working they want to try.
- **Initiative:** They identify problems, run small experiments, examine results, and adjust their work.

This resembles **job crafting**, in which employees actively reshape tasks, relationships, or the meaning of their work (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). Rudolph et al.'s (2017) meta-analysis of 122 samples and 35,670 participants connected job crafting with proactive personality and engagement. Seeking challenging demands was also related to other-rated performance. It did not establish causal effects of generative AI on working hours or performance.

> **DrSong's View · Improvements Waiting to Be Tried**
>
> I am interested in the person who has long thought, “I could improve this if I had the capacity.” AI does not create that person's concern or purpose. It may reduce the effort needed to turn an existing idea into a concrete attempt. Saved time can then become a higher-quality result rather than empty space. Without changes in roles and priorities, however, the improvement may remain one person's additional labor.

## 03 · Work Redesign and Workflow Redesign

| Approach | What is redesigned? | The practical question |
|---|---|---|
| Work redesign | Goals, tasks, responsibilities, authority, capabilities, resources, and performance standards for a role or team. | What should this role achieve, and what should people and AI each be responsible for? |
| Workflow redesign | The stages, sequence, inputs, outputs, AI contributions, and human judgment points that produce a particular result. | How does this work proceed, and where would a change improve the outcome? |

Work redesign establishes who is accountable for which outcomes. Workflow redesign establishes the steps and judgments through which those outcomes are achieved. Changing an entire role at once can be too ambitious. A more practical starting point is one workflow, expanding into role redesign when it requires changes to authority, responsibilities, or resources.

## 04 · Five Steps for Practice

The goal is to connect employees' improvement questions with organizational aims and test whether a small change works.

**1. Choose a concrete task to improve.** Before choosing a tool, ask what employees would improve if they had more time, what disappoints them about current results, and what difference they want to make for customers or colleagues. Select a pilot whose problem and goal can be explained clearly through real examples.

**2. Map the current workflow.** Describe five to eight steps from the starting point to the outcome. Identify participants, time demands, waiting, rework, and judgments that determine quality. Use a precise outcome—for example, drawing decision-relevant evidence and recommendations from five client interviews—rather than a broad label such as report writing.

**3. Measure more than time.** Alongside a goal such as 30% faster drafting, examine accuracy in framing customer problems, use of alternatives in decisions, or fewer overlooked cases. Agree on a baseline, comparison period, scope, and completion criteria. Decide what will be reduced or postponed to make room for improvement.

**4. Reallocate work between people and AI.** Repetitive transcription, classification, and drafting may suit AI. It can also broaden perspectives or analysis options. People establish context and standards, verify evidence, and remain accountable for final judgments. Adding a tool is not itself redesign: remove unhelpful steps, specify review points, and define when the work is complete.

**5. Adjust work and resources in light of the pilot.** Run the new workflow for four to six weeks. Examine time, quality, rework, and improvements actually adopted. If it helps, document the procedure and align authority, learning opportunities, and tools with the role. If work expands without better outcomes, simplify or redesign again. Even a successful pilot should prompt a review of existing priorities and workload before new tasks are added.

## 05 · Organizational Performance Requires Improvements That Stay in the Work

Giving a capable employee more assignments is different from embedding that person's improvement in organizational practice. The former depends on continuing individual effort; the latter shares a tested procedure, reduces repeated work, and can reshape roles and capabilities. An improvement becomes an organizational contribution when colleagues can use it as a repeatable standard or workflow.

AI may save time for some and make better work possible for others. Ask not only how much time remains, but what people can now do better, who wants to make those improvements, and what must change for the benefits to travel beyond the individual. **Connecting employees' questions and AI's capabilities through goals, roles, and workflows is where work redesign begins.**

### Insight Questions

1. What have employees wanted to improve but postponed for lack of time or a workable approach?
2. Do their roles, authority, and workflows reflect that intention and expertise?
3. How can an AI-supported improvement become repeatable organizational performance rather than additional individual labor?
