### Asking About the Organization in a Hospital Full of Experts

This essay draws on a synthesis of healthcare teamwork research (Rosen et al., 2018), reviews of followership among clinicians, and interview studies of the transition into management roles.

It presents the essay “Why Did a Nurse Choose Educational Technology (HRD) Instead of Nursing?” by Hee Je Yun, a nurse at Seoul National University Hospital and a doctoral student in Educational Technology at Hanyang University, in the Weekly Insight format. Drawing on many years as a nurse, the author connects questions raised at the bedside with research read in graduate school to explain why we need to study organizations.

The cited sources differ in kind: a research synthesis, literature reviews, an experiment, surveys, and qualitative interview studies. The interview studies have small samples and describe particular institutions, so please read the research findings and the author's own field experience as separate things.

## 01 · Why Did a Nurse Come to Educational Technology?

Why did you decide to study organizations (organization development, human resource development, and related fields)? Since entering graduate school, I have often been asked, “Why did a nurse go into educational technology instead of nursing?” To be honest, I chose educational technology simply because I was interested in education, without any particular purpose, and I ended up in an organization-related specialization without any particular intention either. Then one day, glancing at the bookshelf in my living room, I noticed a few books I had read years earlier out of personal interest: *Drive* (Daniel Pink), *Nudge* (Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein), and *Switch* (Chip Heath and Dan Heath). When I first read them I paid no attention to the authors, but when I opened them again and read the author bios, I found they were behavioral economists or organizational behavior scholars. That is when it came back to me. Long before I came to graduate school to study organizations, I had been asking myself, “Why do my fellow nurses, doing the same work, think so differently, and sometimes act in opposite ways in the same situation?” and “Why can't we pull together around the same idea and get through a crisis?” As I took courses, did assignments, and read papers in graduate school, I became more certain. This study, the study of organizations, was not only something I had wanted without realizing it, but something truly necessary, and a discipline that everyone who belongs to an organization should study. Unless you run a one-person business that handles everything alone, most people work in organizations that differ only in size, form, structure, and number of members. I believe that working in an organization without studying organizations is no different from going to war without learning how to use a rifle. Whatever your field, wherever you work, and whatever industry your organization belongs to, as long as you belong to an organization, I believe studying the organization is as important as studying your own field.

A hospital is often described as an organization of expert groups. Nurses, physicians, pharmacists, radiologic technologists, clinical laboratory scientists, and others, each with long years of education and training in a particular field, belong to one organization but work in groups organized by their own domains. Over many years as a nurse, I have seen every day how deep their expertise runs, and at the same time I have carried a question I could never resolve. People whose expertise has grown and who have risen to positions of responsibility spare no time in digging deeper into their own fields, yet they hardly study how the organization they belong to actually works, or how they should work together with their colleagues.

What I want to argue in this essay is that, as long as we work in an organization, whether as leaders or followers, seniors or juniors, we should study organization development and human resource development as seriously as our own professional field. I offer three reasons. First, inside an organization even the world's best expert cannot produce results alone; results come only when members move together. Second, even in a follower's position rather than a leader's, you need to understand the theories and mechanisms that make an organization work in order to read how it operates and to find your role and deliver results within it. Third, as experience accumulates, a leadership role arrives, whether formal or informal.

## 02 · One World-Class Physician Does Not Heal a Patient Alone

When a patient is admitted to the hospital with an illness or injury, having one of the world's best physicians does not by itself mean the patient will recover. However well the surgeon operates, if 24-hour observation by nurses after surgery, communication with physicians about changes in the patient's condition, and prompt preparation and dispensing of medications by pharmacists according to the orders do not mesh together, the patient is bound to deteriorate. Synthesizing research on teamwork in healthcare, Rosen et al. (2018) argued that patient safety incidents such as misdiagnosis, medication errors, surgical errors, and hospital-acquired infections can be caused not only by gaps in individual knowledge but also by failures of teamwork. Conversely, activities in which the team moves together, such as interdisciplinary rounds that involve several professions or brief pre-procedure huddles and debriefs, led to better communication and patient outcomes, and evidence-based team training was shown to actually improve teamwork and performance. This can be understood to show that what determines patient outcomes is not the skill of the single best person, but how well the members move together.

Going further, high expertise can itself become a barrier to working together. As expertise in a field grows, the field's way of thinking can harden into cognitive entrenchment, making it harder to respond flexibly to new situations or to accept new rules and beliefs (Dane, 2010). In the experiments of Ottati et al. (2015), people who felt more like experts showed more dogmatic, closed-minded thinking, which supports this negative side of cognitive entrenchment. Even so, the study by Michalec et al. (2024), which surveyed 305 nurses and physicians, offers a hopeful note. According to that study, respondents saw humility as lowering status differences between professions, making team-based collaboration possible, and being essential to high-quality, team-based, patient-centered care. Taken together, these three studies suggest that the problem is not raising expertise itself, but raising only expertise while paying no attention to the organization and to colleagues. That is why we need to study organizations: inside an organization, results are made together, not alone.

## 03 · Followers Need to Study the Organization, Too

When people hear that we should study organizations, they often assume it is only for those who will become managers or leaders. But on reflection, leaders are a small minority of an organization's members; most are followers. And even someone who leads a department is, from the standpoint of the higher levels of the hierarchy, also a follower. Reviewing 42 studies of followership among healthcare clinicians, Alanazi, Wiechula, & Foley (2023) argued that organizations have far more followers than leaders and that how followers behave directly affects team roles, communication, patient safety, and quality of care. In a similar vein, after reviewing 48 studies of followership in interprofessional healthcare teams, Barry, Teunissen, & Varpio (2024) argued that we should move away from seeing followers as passive members who simply carry out a leader's instructions and instead understand followership as an active role that every member performs within shared leadership.

> **Hee Je Yun's View · Followers who understand how the organization works**<br>
> I think this is why followers, too, need to study the organization. When a new guideline or work procedure is rolled out in the hospital, I often see that people who understand why the change is needed now, what process the decision went through, and why people resist change respond completely differently from those who do not, even when they receive the same instruction. The former, rather than letting frustration build, read the intent of the change and look for the part they can play; they try the new procedure, and if they see a problem, they raise their view upward in an appropriate way and through the proper channel. Followers who understand how the organization works know when and how to speak up and how to build trust with colleagues, so they will be able to achieve more from their own position. Theories and models of organization development and human resource development are not tools for leaders alone; they can guide everyone who works in an organization to understand their role and carry it out effectively.

## 04 · Leadership Roles Arrive Whether You Want Them or Not

If studying the organization as a follower is the starting point, the need to study it grows as experience accumulates. In clinical practice, as your experience grows, a leadership role (formal or informal) arrives whether you want it or not. In the study by Imran et al. (2021), interviews with 18 physicians holding management positions at a large Australian hospital found that some had taken on management roles under pressure even though they had not wanted them, and many felt unprepared for the role. In the study by Pilat & Merriam (2019), which interviewed 10 staff nurses who had become nurse managers, participants likewise experienced role insufficiency amid a lack of support and resources. A study of 93 nurse champions (Luz et al., 2019) found that informal champions, who led change on their own initiative without a title, had longer tenure than formal champions, showing that leadership is not confined to formal positions.

> **Hee Je Yun's View · Filling the leadership gap yourself**<br>
> Being given a title does not mean that subordinates or junior staff will recognize you as a real leader and willingly follow. Working in a hospital, I often see that even when one person has been assigned a leadership role, junior staff who run into difficulty go first not to that person but to another senior colleague who holds no formal title or leadership role. Leadership can be handed to you regardless of your wishes, and for that very reason there are few chances to prepare for it and learn it. Simply attending leadership lectures or workshops after a leadership role is given will probably not make someone able to lead in the full sense. But a person who has studied the organization since their days as a follower will be able to fill that gap on their own when a leadership role arrives or is assigned.

## 05 · People Who Work in Organizations Must Learn About Organizations

The reason to study organizations is not only that I may become a leader. It is that inside an organization even the world's best expert cannot produce results alone; everyone has to move together. Leaders need to understand the organization in order to lead members into motion, and followers need to understand it in order to grasp and carry out their own roles within that motion, each from their own position. That, I believe, is why I, and we, are pursuing this study now. My fellow graduate students in the lab, myself included, are already experts in our own fields. That we have chosen to study organizations and people, and are working our way through this demanding process while juggling work and study, is probably because every day in our workplaces we face problems that expertise alone cannot explain or solve.

> **Hee Je Yun's View · Why a nurse studies organizations**<br>
> So from now on, this is how I will answer the question, “Why did a nurse come to educational technology?” I study organizations because I am a nurse who works in an organization called a hospital. Just as someone going to war learns to fire a rifle, someone who works in an organization must learn about organizations.
