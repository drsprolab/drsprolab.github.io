### The Capability Mirage behind the Perfect Report

Swift, M., Andreu, T., & Hernandez, D. (2026, September 14). How AI creates a capability mirage. *MIT Sloan Management Review*.

The authors' **capability mirage** describes a situation in which polished AI-assisted work is mistaken for an improvement in employees' underlying capability. Authors may fail to recognize errors, and colleagues may miss them too. A report on which everyone agrees, but whose reasoning nobody can explain, illustrates the problem.

The authors suggest examining what the employee understands and has learned alongside the quality of the output. Their article is a management analysis informed by expert interviews, not an experiment establishing that AI causes a decline in capability.
</div>

## 01 · What Is a Capability Mirage?

Swift and colleagues (2026) ask whether organizational capability might be weakening unnoticed as AI makes it harder to judge what individuals can actually do.

Drawing on expert concerns raised in Anthrome Insight and Axialent's work on AI's behavioral and cultural effects, they use **capability mirage** to describe an apparently capable workforce whose human expertise may be eroding behind the outputs. Their imagery includes dry rot, a Potemkin village, and the curtain concealing the Wizard of Oz: a reassuring surface can conceal a less dependable foundation.

In the interview account, Aestora CEO Stephanie Antonian observes that widespread access to competent-looking outputs does not reveal what lies beneath them, how robust the work is, or what additional risks it carries.

## 02 · Why the Mirage Appears

**Outputs alone no longer distinguish capability reliably.** AI can help many people produce polished work. Leaders may struggle to separate an employee's expertise from the tool's contribution. This is one way to understand **performance level ≠ learning level**.

**Errors may escape both author and reviewer.** A fluent, well-organized document can seem convincing. If its author does not recognize a problem, a reviewer may also overlook it.

**Output quality and human capability can move in different directions.** Work may remain good or improve while people become less able to produce and verify it independently. That change need not be visible in the finished document. The familiar assumption that better performance necessarily implies deeper learning breaks down.

## 03 · The Authors' Proposal: Evaluate Learning alongside Outputs

Swift and colleagues (2026) propose checking whether employees understand the work and what they learned in producing it. As AI narrows differences between outputs, outputs alone become a less adequate basis for recognizing and rewarding capability.

**A note on the evidence:** The source is expert interview-based management analysis. It raises a diagnostic concern, rather than experimentally demonstrating that AI use causes capability loss.

## 04 · Questions Worth Taking Further

Imagine an employee submitting an AI-assisted report that a manager reviews and approves. In the meeting, someone asks why the conclusion follows. Neither author nor reviewer can explain the reasoning. The document has passed review, but its completeness has hidden a gap in understanding.

> **DrSong's View · From a Learning Perspective**
>
> Learning should be assessed less by what a person encountered than by what changed following the experience (Alexander et al., 2009; Illeris, 2018). Reading and submitting an AI-generated report does not in itself establish a change in how someone sees a problem or makes a judgment. If the internal work of connecting a new experience with prior knowledge and making sense of it is skipped, the output may remain while learning does not.

> **DrSong's View · From an Organizational Learning Perspective**
>
> Agreement alone is insufficient. In Crossan and colleagues' (1999) 4I framework, an individual's insight must pass through interpreting—putting it into words—and integrating—examining the reasoning and developing shared understanding—to become organizational. Agreement without an ability to explain its basis is closer to a shared mirage than shared understanding. If that report is institutionalized as a template or decision rule, the organization may preserve an unexamined judgment.

### Insight Questions

1. Does our evaluation distinguish the quality of the output from the author's ability to explain it?
2. When reviewing AI drafts, do reviewers ask about the basis of judgments rather than presentation alone?
3. How can we tell whether employees have become better at judging the work or merely better at using the tool?
