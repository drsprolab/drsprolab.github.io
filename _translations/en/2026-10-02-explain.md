**Key Takeaway**

AI can help produce an excellent report without leaving its author able to explain the reasoning behind it. The task is complete, but little may remain with the person who completed it. This is what **performance level ≠ learning level** can look like at work.

## AI Paradox 1

## 01 · When Someone Asked Why, Nobody Had an Answer

Imagine a monthly management meeting. The strategy team presents its priorities for entering Southeast Asian markets. The report is clear and polished: six countries, five evaluation criteria, and a weighted score establishing the order of entry.

An executive asks not for the conclusion, but for **the reasoning behind it**. Why does market size carry a 30% weight? Why are relationships with key clients absent from the criteria? Neither the employee who drafted the report nor the manager who reviewed it can answer. The weights came with the evaluation framework proposed by generative AI. Earlier in the same meeting, HRD had reported the success of its AI training: 98% completion and a 40% reduction in report-writing time.

## 02 · The Trap of Feeling That We Understand an Output

People tend to believe they understand how things work more deeply than they actually do. Rozenblit and Keil (2002) called this the **illusion of explanatory depth**. Familiarity makes us feel we know something; being asked to explain it step by step reveals the limits of that knowledge.

AI can reinforce this illusion. We have read and edited a report several times, so we feel that we understand it. Yet we did not develop the reasoning that supports its judgments. Learning science distinguishes **performance** on a current task from **learning** that lasts, and shows why smooth, effortless performance can be a misleading indicator of learning (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). Conversely, activities that require people to explain something for themselves have consistently been associated with deeper understanding (Bisra et al., 2018).

## 03 · How AI Can Reinforce the Illusion

In an experiment involving 117 university students, learners who wrote with ChatGPT showed the greatest improvement in the quality of their outputs, but did not differ from the other groups in knowledge acquisition or transfer. The authors warned of **metacognitive laziness**: relying on AI while investing less effort in planning and monitoring one's own learning (Fan et al., 2025). When automated systems appear dependable, people can also fall prey to **automation bias**, checking less carefully and overlooking errors (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

The author feels that they understand; the reviewer is reassured by a polished presentation. Between them, the output can improve while the human capability needed to produce and defend it weakens. This is why output quality alone tells us so little about learning.

## 04 · Check Whether Judgment Survives behind the Output

**1. Add three lines of reasoning to the reporting template.** Below an AI-assisted report's conclusion, ask the author to explain in three lines why that conclusion follows. HRD can provide the template, and managers can make a completed reasoning section a condition of review.

**2. Establish two explanation questions for review meetings.** Whenever AI has contributed to a report, ask:

> “Who chose this number, weight, or criterion—and why?”
>
> “If this conclusion were wrong, where would the error most likely be?”

**3. Change the final 20 minutes of AI training.** After learners submit their work, turn off AI and ask them to explain their decision process to a partner for three minutes. The instructor can record a simple assessment: explains the reasoning, explains it partially, or cannot explain it.

**4. Add one learning indicator to the performance report.** Alongside course completion and time savings, report **the proportion of learners who can explain the reasoning in their own report 30 days after training**. Asking five managers to check two reports each offers a small, practical way to begin within a month.

### Insight Questions

1. Does our team evaluate the quality of an output separately from its author's ability to explain it?
2. When reviewing AI-assisted drafts, do we ask about the reasoning behind decisions, rather than primarily the presentation?
3. How could we tell whether employees are making better judgments, rather than simply becoming more proficient with the tool?
