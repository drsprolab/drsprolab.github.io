<div class="daily-takeaway" markdown="1">
**Key Takeaway**

How we define learning determines where we think learning remains and what we are willing to recognize as learning. Dewey saw learning as the reconstruction of an individual's experience. Organizational learning theory moved that location to the organization's information processing and memory; the learning-organization and practice perspectives moved it to participation and culture among people; and the knowledge-creation perspective extended it to artifacts that people refine together. All of these definitions share one set of assumptions: **the learner is a person, knowledge is someone's belief, and the learning process can be observed among people.** Generative AI and collaborative spaces such as GitHub unsettle all three at once. Applying the old definitions unchanged therefore overestimates some learning and renders other learning invisible. For the AI era, I propose defining learning as a process in which human judgment and shared artifacts change together, leaving evidence and a history that allow the change to be questioned again, and defining knowledge as a shared belief that has been verified in this way and that someone takes responsibility for using.

### Changing the Definition Changes Which Learning We Can See

Dewey (1916), read alongside eleven other sources.

Instead of a single paper, this essay follows a lineage of definitions. It starts from Dewey's philosophy of experience and reads Argyris's double-loop learning, Huber's information-processing view, Nonaka's knowledge creation, and the 4I model of Crossan et al. It then turns to Sfard and Paavola et al. on metaphors of learning, Elkjaer, who brought Dewey back into organizational learning, Marsick and Watkins, who measured the learning organization, and Song and Chermack (2008), who linked individual learning to learning-organization culture, and summarizes what each definition made it possible to explain.

Most of the literature discussed is theoretical, and none of it tests the definitions themselves empirically. The application of these definitions to generative AI environments, and the redefinition that follows, are this essay's interpretation and proposal.
</div>

## 01 · A Definition Determines What Can Be Seen

Defining learning requires deciding three things: who learns (the subject), where what has been learned remains (the location of knowledge), and what confirms that learning has occurred (the evidence). Only when these are settled can learning be measured, and only when it can be measured can education and organization development be designed. Why has HRD argued over the definition of learning for so long? Each time the definition changed, results that had previously gone unseen came to count as learning, and new practices emerged to produce them. Huber's definition became a basis for knowledge management systems, and the definition of the learning organization led to surveys that measure learning culture.

## 02 · From Dewey to Knowledge Creation: A Lineage of Definitions

Dewey (1916) defined education as **the reconstruction or reorganization of experience**, which adds to the meaning of experience and increases the ability to direct the course of later experience. For Dewey, learning is the work of inquiring into a blocked situation and rearranging thought and action. Building on this definition, later theories gradually moved the place where learning resides.

| Perspective | Definition of learning | Definition of knowledge | What only this definition can explain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Experience (Dewey, 1916) | Reconstruction or reorganization of experience | The result of inquiry and a tool for the next action | Counts problem solving and reflection in the course of work as learning → roots of experiential and workplace learning |
| Error correction (Argyris, 1976) | Detecting and correcting errors. Correcting only the action is single-loop; correcting the governing variables beneath it is double-loop | The theory of action that guides behavior | Explains why the same mistakes recur and why defensive routines persist |
| Information processing (Huber, 1991) | Learning occurs when processing information changes the range of potential behaviors | Information that has been acquired, distributed, and interpreted and is held in organizational memory | An organization can be said to have learned even before behavior changes → basis for knowledge management systems |
| Knowledge creation (Nonaka, 1994) | A process in which tacit and explicit knowledge convert into each other and knowledge grows in a spiral | Justified true belief | The path by which an individual's know-how becomes organizational knowledge, and the creation of new knowledge |
| 4I (Crossan et al., 1999) | A process moving across individual, group, and organization: intuiting → interpreting → integrating → institutionalizing | From individual cognition to institutionalized routines | The boundary where individual learning becomes organizational learning (institutionalization), and the tension between exploration and exploitation |
| Participation and inquiry (Sfard, 1998; Elkjaer, 2004) | From possessing (acquisition) to participating in practice, and then to inquiry within a social world | Resides in practice | Counts communities of practice, dialogue, and working together outside formal training as learning |
| Learning organization (Marsick & Watkins, 2003; Song & Chermack, 2008) | A culture that supports continuous learning and the capacity to change oneself. The process by which individual learning becomes a shared belief | The organization's knowledge as a shared belief | Measures learning culture across seven dimensions and links it to performance; explains individual learning and organizational culture as one process |
| Shared object (Paavola et al., 2004) | Jointly developing a common object (a document, model, or design) | Artifacts and practices that people refine together | The process by which a group creates new knowledge in a collaborative space |

Huber's (1991) definition made it possible to speak of learning even when behavior has not changed, and as a result organizational memory and knowledge management systems entered the picture as parts of learning. Sfard (1998) divided learning into two metaphors, “acquisition” and “participation,” and warned against choosing only one, and Paavola et al. (2004) added a third, “knowledge creation,” which develops a shared object together. Elkjaer (2004) brought Dewey's concept of inquiry back to explain organizational learning as a “third way” that is neither acquisition nor participation.

## 03 · Three Assumptions the Definitions Have Shared

Although the definitions differ, they share assumptions they have relied on in common. First, the learner is a person. Even when we say that an organization learns, we mean that a collection of people learns. Second, knowledge is someone's belief. Nonaka's (1994) justified belief and Song and Chermack's (2008) shared belief both presuppose someone who believes. Third, the learning process can be observed among people. There were scenes that researchers and practitioners could see: conversations that correct errors, meetings that align interpretations, and institutionalized procedures.

Generative AI and cloud collaboration spaces such as GitHub and Second Brain unsettle these three assumptions at once. AI now handles much of what Huber (1991) identified as the processes of organizational learning: acquiring, distributing, and interpreting information. A draft produced by AI enters a repository without anyone having believed it, and becomes someone else's answer. As an individual's notes and commits become organizational knowledge directly, without passing through conversation among people, the scenes of learning have [hidden themselves inside the algorithm](/weekly-insight/2026-10-27-knowledge-management-ai/).

## 04 · Can the Old Definitions Interpret Learning in the AI Era?

Each definition divides into what it explains well in an AI environment and what it does not.

| Definition | What it explains in AI and collaborative spaces | What it fails to explain or overestimates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reconstruction of experience (Dewey) | Inquiring into a blocked problem together with AI can reconstruct experience | If AI resolves the blocked situation first, inquiry never begins. Cognitive offloading, which passes the cognitive burden to a tool (Risko & Gilbert, 2016), is the channel |
| Error correction (Argyris) | PR reviews, reverts, and revision histories are records of error detection and correction | Governing variables are embedded in prompts and model defaults, so it is unclear what should be asked |
| Information processing (Huber) | The organization's range of potential behavior clearly expands | By this definition, “learned” holds even if no person's understanding changes. It is the definition that overestimates most |
| Knowledge creation (Nonaka) | AI quickly takes over combination, the weaving together of explicit knowledge | In justified belief, the person who justifies drops out. Experiences in which tacit knowledge could accumulate decrease |
| 4I (Crossan et al.) | Integration and institutionalization (merging, version control) are actually more visible | The intuiting and interpreting stages are skipped or taken over by AI, which blurs whose learning has been institutionalized |
| Participation and shared object (Sfard, Paavola et al.) | Collaboration on GitHub comes closest to jointly developing a shared object | Does not answer whether AI should be regarded as a participant, or whose share it is in advancing the object |

The old definitions illuminate an AI environment only one side at a time. Seen through system-side definitions (Huber, Nonaka's combination, 4I's institutionalization), organizations appear to learn faster than before; seen through person-side definitions (Dewey, Argyris, shared belief), the core scene of learning is emptying out. Raisch and Krakowski (2021) argued that automation and augmentation around AI are intertwined, so pursuing only one of them creates problems, and the same holds for defining learning. If we choose only one side's definition, we cannot see the losses occurring on the other.

**What to keep in mind now · Nature of the evidence:** The table above is my own analysis, applying each source's definition to a generative AI environment. The original authors did not write their definitions with generative AI in mind. Raisch and Krakowski (2021) is also a conceptual paper, and empirical studies showing how organizational learning changes in AI collaboration spaces are still few.

## 05 · If We Were to Redefine Learning and Knowledge

> **DrSong's View · Defining Learning and Knowledge**
> I propose that learning and knowledge in the AI era be redefined as follows.
>
> **Knowledge** is information that people and AI have produced together, which people have weighed against evidence and accepted as a shared belief, which someone takes responsibility for using, and for which a history remains so that it can be corrected again. Being stored in a repository does not make something knowledge. It is knowledge only if we can answer the question, “Whose knowledge is it?”
>
> **Learning** is a process, within a system where people and AI work together, in which people's understanding and judgment and the shared artifacts change together, and the evidence and history of that change remain so that it can be questioned again. If we look only at the human side, we cannot explain what was achieved together with AI; if we look only at the artifact side, we mistake an organization in which no one has learned for a learning organization.
>
> This definition is also a way back to Dewey. For Dewey, learning was increasing the ability to direct the course of later experience. In an environment where AI supplies answers, that ability is closer to the capacity to doubt an answer, to ask again about the criteria, and to record the reasons than to the capacity to produce answers. If we move the three layers I proposed at the individual level in the earlier essay [Redefining Learning in the AI Era](/weekly-insight/2026-10-22-redefining-learning-ai/) (understanding that remains without AI, capability working with AI, and the judgment to notice AI's errors) up to the organizational level, we arrive at this definition.

## 06 · What HRD Can Do

- **Check learning in three places:** Look together at people (can they explain it without AI?), artifacts (what was revised?), and the records that connect the two (who approved what, and on what grounds?).
- **Leave the blocked situation in place:** Before asking AI, have people define the problem first and write down an expected answer. Dewey's inquiry begins with a block.
- **Design review as a scene of learning:** In code or document review, ask for the grounds before the conclusion, and have people record disagreements and the reasons for revisions. This is where the conversation and negotiation that had disappeared become visible again.
- **Use metrics that fit the definition:** The number of stored documents or the volume of AI use are metrics that follow only a Huber-style definition. Look as well at the rate of revision and rejection, the number of follow-up questions, and the rate of explaining without AI.

### Insight Questions

1. How does our organization define learning: **who learns, where it remains, and what confirms it**?
2. Do our learning metrics look at **only one side, either changes in people or changes in artifacts**?
3. For each piece of knowledge accumulated in our repositories, can we answer, **“Whose knowledge is it?”**
