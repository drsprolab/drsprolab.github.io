**Key Takeaway**

When we judge researchers' development only by their publication count, we can lose sight of what they are curious about and what they have learned. At the beginning of a research career, learning to ask questions, understand theories and methods, and explain ideas in one's own words comes first. AI can extend that learning, but it should not take over the reasons for the research or the judgments it requires. Publication is a way to share ideas formed through study and refined through criticism and revision.

### From Studying to Publish to Studying to Pursue a Question

This essay draws on commentary and critique about research productivity, a conference panel on AI in academic research, a literature review, and qualitative interviews with 29 HRD researchers and scholar-practitioners.

These sources differ in purpose and method. Bringing them into conversation with a researcher's personal reflections, the essay asks what researchers should study and who they want to become under publication pressure. It offers a perspective and a set of questions, rather than a universal prescription for every discipline or evidence of a causal effect.
</div>

## 01 · Publishing Is Important. It Is Not the Purpose of Research.

“Publish or Perish” captures a familiar reality of academic life. Publishing is an important way to communicate findings and expose them to a scholarly community's scrutiny. Yet when publication counts, journal impact, and citation metrics stand in for achievement, the volume of output can overshadow what has been discovered and why it matters to others.

In a commentary on publication culture in academic surgery, Moris and colleagues discuss how quantitative indicators may shape the definition and reward of academic excellence. Their article is commentary on a particular field, not an empirical measurement of every researcher's experience. Even so, it leaves us with a useful question: should publication count and research value be treated as the same thing? What matters is not simply how much we publish, but which problems we examine closely and what we hope to share.

## 02 · Using AI Makes Researchers' Questions and Judgment More Important

Generative AI can help organize literature, structure ideas, and refine expression. But researchers must continue to judge what they want to know, why a problem matters, and which evidence to accept. If they hand those questions and perspectives to a tool as well, outputs may arrive faster without a comparable accumulation of learning.

The conference panel involving Cha and colleagues considers the productive possibilities of integrating AI into research alongside concerns about cognitive atrophy, declining skills, authorship, and graduate education. This is a discussion of researchers' perspectives and concerns, not an experiment demonstrating these risks. The useful distinction is between tasks that can be delegated and the thinking and verification for which people remain responsible.

> **Jaehong Joo's View · AI as a Way to Take Thinking Further**
>
> I see a worthwhile use of AI as helping researchers take their thinking further, rather than thinking in their place. We need sufficient grounding in a subject to judge whether an AI answer is sound, plausible but mistaken, or capable of opening a different perspective.

## 03 · Early in a Research Career, Study Comes First

Manabu's theoretical critique presents becoming a scholar as more than accumulating knowledge. It involves joining an academic community through reading, summarizing, writing, revising, and reviewing. These surrounding tasks are not merely administrative burdens; they can also be an apprenticeship in scholarly judgment. The argument is theoretical rather than an experimental test of learning effects, but it invites us to reconsider the value of early research experiences.

“Study or Leave” is therefore not an instruction to leave the lab. It is an invitation to ask ourselves why we remain if we are not learning. Coming to understand a research question, making a theory or method one's own, or learning to explain a problem in one's own language is a meaningful achievement.

## 04 · We Persist So That Our Ideas Can Reach Other People

A paper is rarely completed in one attempt. It may be rejected or returned with requests for substantial revision. A problem the author regards as important may be understood differently by readers. In their literature review, Teixeira da Silva and Nazarovets discuss the pressures of peer review and the burdens it can place on researchers, emphasizing resilience in responding to evaluation. We do not need to romanticize these difficulties. Criticism and revision can nevertheless help us clarify our thinking and communicate it to others.

In HRD, the relationship between research and practice also matters. Moore and Cho interviewed 29 HRD scholars and scholar-practitioners about the research–practice gap, barriers to collaboration, and ways to narrow the gap. Participants suggested developing research agendas with practitioners from the outset and connecting scholarly rigor with practical application. The interviews do not demonstrate effectiveness across all organizations, but they underscore the importance of asking early who will help shape a research question and with whom its findings will be shared.

> **Jaehong Joo's View · From My Research to Our Knowledge**
>
> When ideas I have struggled with, read about, written, and revised reach other people—and are reshaped through their practical questions—research becomes more than an individual's output. It becomes shared knowledge. That is why I want to locate its purpose in sharing what we have learned with the world, rather than in publication itself.

### Three Things to Try in the Lab

- **Keep a record of your questions.** Write a sentence stating what you want to know and why it matters.
- **Explain what you learn in your own words.** Connect a theory or method to an actual problem, and make a note of what you do not yet understand.
- **Talk with practitioners early.** Ask whether the research question speaks to concerns they experience, and share the findings in a form they can understand and use.

Writing and publishing papers matter. But study should be their starting point. As AI makes text easier to produce, I want to keep asking why I am studying a problem—and to keep refining the answer.

### Insight Questions

1. What is the research question that is distinctly mine?
2. Is AI helping my research, or thinking in my place?
3. Which theory, method, or topic do I most want to make my own during my time in the lab?
