**Key Takeaway**

AI makes it possible to create and access training on demand. Yet an organization can be surrounded by learning content without learning much itself. What individuals consume alone may never change how people work together. HRD needs to design the pathways through which learning stays with the organization; producing more content is already something AI can do.
</div>

## AI Paradox 10

## 01 · Fewer Hours in Class, Better Conversations at Work

Imagine an HRD team dramatically expanding internal courses with AI. Participation hours increase, but operational leaders see little change.

The next year, HRD takes a different approach. It halves the formal course schedule and uses some of that time to facilitate thirty-minute debriefs in real project meetings: What did we try? What did we learn? What should change next time? In this illustrative case, teams begin changing procedures, and colleagues in other teams start adapting those practices for their own work.

## 02 · How Individual Learning Becomes Organizational

Crossan and colleagues' (1999) framework describes four interrelated learning processes: **intuiting**, in which individuals recognize a possibility; **interpreting**, in which they put it into words and share it; **integrating**, in which people develop a shared understanding and coordinated action; and **institutionalizing**, in which learning becomes embedded in organizational rules and routines.

However much an individual learns, learning may remain personal without interpretation and integration. Saying that an organization has learned implies a change in its practices.

## 03 · Content Alone Cannot Complete the Process

AI-personalized content can enrich individuals' insights and possibilities. The other processes depend on interaction among people and engagement with actual work. Workplace learning occurs not only through planned training but also through work and conversations with colleagues (Noe et al., 2014).

**Debriefing** is one well-supported way of learning from that work. A meta-analysis found that systematic debriefs improved individual and team effectiveness by roughly 25% compared with control conditions (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli, 2013). For a debrief to support learning, people also need the psychological safety to discuss mistakes and questions candidly (Edmondson & Lei, 2014). Abundant content can, paradoxically, crowd out time for the interpretation and integration that matter here.

## 04 · Turn Learning into the Way the Team Works

**1. Reallocate some teaching time to facilitating workplace debriefs.** An HRD practitioner can join three or four teams' project meetings each month for a thirty-minute reflection. Use three questions: What did we try? What differed from expectations? What will we change next time?

**2. Keep a one-page change record.** In a shared team space, record what will change, why, and from when. As these records accumulate, institutionalization becomes more visible.

**3. Hold a quarterly session for teams to borrow from one another.** Give each team ten minutes to explain one practice it has changed, so that others can adapt it. This helps learning travel beyond its original team.

**4. Change HRD's performance indicators.** Alongside—or in place of—course counts and participation hours, report how many working procedures changed through debriefs that quarter and how many spread to other teams.

### Insight Questions

1. How many examples do we have from last quarter of individual learning changing an actual working procedure?
2. Do HRD practitioners spend more time in classrooms or in conversations about real work?
3. Has the increase in AI-generated content expanded or reduced time for interpretation and integration?
