**Key Takeaway**

An answer from AI does not mean that a person has learned. Saving that answer does not mean that an organization possesses knowledge, either. From an HRD perspective, knowledge begins with people learning and understanding. It becomes organizational knowledge when colleagues examine it together, apply it in their work, and embed it in shared standards of judgment and practice. AI can support this process, or allow parts of it to be skipped. Sometimes the learning has occurred but is difficult to see in the finished output. We therefore need to ask again what we mean by knowledge in the age of AI.
</div>

<div class="daily-brief" markdown="1">
### Between AI Outputs and Knowledge Formed through Learning

Wiles et al. (2026), a randomized experiment distinguishing AI-assisted task performance from individual knowledge acquisition, read alongside five other sources.

This essay examines the difference between performance achieved with AI and knowledge acquired through learning. Recent studies in *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, *Organization Science*, and *Nature Human Behaviour* suggest that AI can improve task performance and, in some circumstances, support learning. These outcomes do not invariably occur together.

The studies directly examine individual performance and learning. The suggestion that AI may cause organizational learning to be bypassed or become harder to observe is an interpretation informed by organizational learning theory, rather than a finding established by these experiments. That distinction matters throughout this essay.
</div>

## 01 · A Good Output Is Not Evidence of Learning

AI can produce an analysis or a draft quickly. Yet a convincing output leaves important questions unanswered. What has its author come to understand? What has the team learned together? Will this work change how they judge a similar situation next time? The quality of the document alone cannot tell us.

Wiles and colleagues (2026) asked 487 consultants to complete data science tasks. Participants using ChatGPT performed better on coding, statistics, and prediction tasks. On technical questions answered afterward without AI, however, the researchers did not find that this group had acquired more knowledge. Performing a task well with AI and learning from that task may be different achievements. The participants worked for a single consulting firm, which also supported the experiment. The appropriate lesson is therefore to examine performance and learning separately, rather than to generalize these findings to every occupation.

The study does not show that people cannot learn when they use AI. It measured performance on particular tasks and responses to technical questions shortly afterward. It did not examine long-term learning or organizational learning within teams. What it does show is that work outputs alone are an insufficient basis for judging what employees have learned.

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025), meanwhile, provide evidence that AI can support learning. They analyzed the gradual introduction of generative AI among 5,172 customer support agents at one company. The number of customer issues resolved per hour increased by an average of 15%. Less experienced agents improved both the speed and quality of their responses. The authors also reported evidence of learning and improvements in English fluency among some agents. Benefits were smaller for experienced agents, with slight declines on some quality measures. One interpretation is that AI can help newer employees learn by making experienced colleagues' ways of responding more accessible. These were medium-term findings from customer support work in a single firm, a boundary worth keeping in view.

The two studies reveal different possibilities. AI may help people learn, but performance achieved with AI is not guaranteed to become knowledge they retain. Task performance, individual learning, and organizational knowledge are connected; each still needs to be examined in its own right.

## 02 · In HRD, Knowledge Takes Shape through Learning

Song and Chermack (2008) offered a theoretical account of how individual learning develops into organizational knowledge through reflection and interaction. Crossan, Lane, and White (1999) described a progression in which individual intuition and interpretation become shared understanding and, eventually, established organizational practices and institutions. Lim, Song, and Yoon (2014) likewise examined how knowledge management and organizational learning work together to improve workplace performance.

In this view, knowledge is more than information recorded in a document. People need to understand it, explain it, and be able to put it to use. They also need opportunities to reconcile interpretations with colleagues and allow what they learn to change working standards and practice. Recording and sharing documents can help that process. Neither action completes it.

We might therefore define knowledge from an HRD perspective as follows: **Knowledge is what a person has learned to understand, judge, and use. Organizational knowledge exists when that learning has been examined among members and embedded in repeatable standards and practices.** An AI-generated answer can be a starting point for learning. Whether people have understood, applied, and collectively examined it is a separate question.

> **DrSong's View · We Need a Broader Definition of Knowledge**
>
> I find it insufficient to define knowledge in HRD solely as what individuals can recall. It is equally problematic to treat everything AI produces as knowledge. We should distinguish understanding and judgment acquired through individual learning, capabilities developed in human–AI work, and knowledge that colleagues have examined and established as organizational practice. We also need to examine the connections between them. Knowing where an AI-generated document is stored tells us little about who learned what. To explain learning and organizational learning in the AI era, we need to consider who forms knowledge, through what processes, and with what capacity to act again in future work.

## 03 · Has Organizational Learning Disappeared, or Become Harder to See?

Two situations are easy to overlook after AI enters a workflow.

The first is **a process being bypassed**. If an AI answer is saved without examination and reused unchanged by the next person, the organization may accumulate material while missing the conversations through which people align interpretations and revise their judgments. Having an answer in a system is different from having learned it as an organization.

The second is **a process becoming less visible**. Learning may occur as people reformulate questions to AI, check an answer's basis, and incorporate colleagues' views. When those interactions are distributed across people and tools, however, the final document may not reveal who learned what. An invisible process is not necessarily an absent one.

A field experiment by Dell'Acqua and colleagues, published in *Organization Science* in 2026, involved 758 consultants. Across 18 knowledge-work tasks within AI's capabilities, AI users worked faster and produced higher-quality outputs. On a strategy task beyond that capability boundary, they were less likely to reach the correct answer. Even within a single occupation, AI's contribution can vary from one stage of work to another.

The study did not measure knowledge formation or organizational learning. It nevertheless raises a practical question: rather than deciding whether to hand an entire job to AI, we should identify where AI is helpful and where human understanding and judgment remain necessary. Following the workflow can also help us distinguish learning that was skipped from learning that occurred without appearing in the finished product.

## 04 · Designing Only for Less Work Is Not Enough

When AI saves time, discussion often turns to what people should do with the time released. But time savings do not determine the next task. Some people may gain breathing room; others may find that additional work fills the gap. For someone with clear goals and a sustained interest in doing better work, AI may make it possible to pursue improvements previously postponed for lack of time or resources. The scope of their work—and the time they invest in it—may then expand.

In the *Organization Science* study discussed above, AI users completed tasks within AI's capabilities more quickly and achieved higher throughput within a defined task set. The researchers did not investigate whether employees independently expanded their work with the time saved, or whether their actual working hours increased. The study cannot be cited as evidence that AI increases work. It does, however, invite us to consider the choices available in work redesign: achieving an existing goal in less time, improving quality with the capacity released, or attempting a new task.

The possibility of longer working hours is not an established research finding here. It is a possibility I raise when thinking about organizational workflows. People with clear performance goals, a desire for better results, and a habit of asking how their work could improve may find it easier to turn those aspirations into action with AI. More careful analysis and new experiments can expand the work. If an organization merely adds assignments to such employees, their desire to improve may turn into overwork. Workflow redesign needs to specify the time and resources available for improvement, as well as what is to be improved.

The following sequence can help organizations examine a workflow:

1. **Agree on goals for performance and learning.** Clarify whether the aim is greater accuracy, customer value, or a new interpretation. Also identify the judgments the employee should learn and the standards the team should come to share.
2. **Examine the stages of work.** Drafting, comparing material, and generating hypotheses may be suitable tasks to try with AI. People remain accountable for interpreting context, checking evidence, and reconciling stakeholders' interests. AI's capabilities vary within a single workflow.
3. **Check what individuals have learned.** Ask people to examine the main evidence and uncertainties rather than simply accept the AI answer. Can they explain important decisions in their own words? Can they ask better questions or make better judgments on a similar task next time? Repeating the entire task from scratch without AI is not the only way to assess learning.
4. **Choose what the team needs to examine together.** Not every output needs a meeting. Select judgments that affect colleagues' work or will be reused, review them with peers, and briefly record what was accepted or revised.
5. **Embed what has been verified in working practices.** Incorporate effective approaches into standards and procedures, then check whether performance actually improves. Agree with employees whether the capacity released should support further improvement or a reduction in workload.

The purpose of workflow redesign is not to make every task take longer. It is to reduce time spent on repetitive work while making room for improvements that employees want to pursue and the organization needs. What to reduce, and what to develop further, should be decided in relation to both organizational performance goals and employees' learning goals.

### Questions to Discuss at Work

1. Have we discussed with employees which performance or learning goals should benefit from the time AI releases?
2. Where in the workflow do individuals understand an AI answer and teams examine it before adopting it as a working standard?
3. Have people who want to improve their work actually been given time to do so?
