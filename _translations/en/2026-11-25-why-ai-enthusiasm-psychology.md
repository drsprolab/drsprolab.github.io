**Key Takeaway**

Enthusiasm for generative AI is not simply because it finishes work quickly. AI satisfies three different psychological motives at once: the wish to get out of a state of not knowing, the wish to lighten the burden of complex thinking, and the wish to feel like someone who can accomplish things. **Telling the three apart makes both AI's appeal and its risks clearer, and opens the way to designs that turn the same motives into learning.**

### A Definite Answer, the Burden of Thinking, and Feeling Competent

Kruglanski, A. W., & Webster, D. M. (1996); Risko, E. F., & Gilbert, S. J. (2016); Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000); Bastani, H., et al. (2025); Aslanov, I., et al. (2025).
</div>

## 01 · Just Because It Is Convenient? Psychological Needs People Can Hardly Help

Why are we so enthusiastic about generative AI? If we simply conclude that it is because AI makes things comfortable, takes less labor, and produces better results, we cannot build alternatives or countermeasures. Only by thinking about why in several ways and interpreting it from multiple angles can we build strategies for using generative AI positively.

So I looked at it from the perspectives of the human need for cognitive closure, offloading (a mechanism by which the brain pursues efficiency), and the basic psychological need to feel competent, and organized what I found from my own viewpoint. Generative AI satisfies three different psychological motives at once. The first is the wish to “get out of not knowing quickly.” The second is the wish to “reduce the burden of thinking things through.” The third is the wish to “feel like someone who can accomplish something.”

The three look alike but differ. The first concerns the discomfort of uncertainty, the second the effort that thinking requires, and the third how we feel about and judge our own abilities. Separating them makes both AI's appeal and its danger clearer.

## 02 · Wanting a Definite Answer: Escaping the Discomfort of Not Knowing

When we receive a hard task, we bear two burdens. One is having to do the work; the other is the discomfort of not knowing what to do or how. The first mechanism explains the latter. It is not that the work is hard; the unsettled state, with no answer yet, is itself uncomfortable.

The **need for cognitive closure** that Kruglanski and Webster (1996) described is a motive to want a clear answer rather than ambiguity. As it rises, people try to reach a conclusion as quickly as possible and tend to hold on to a conclusion once they have it. That also raises the chance of ending judgment before exploring enough.

Generative AI reduces this discomfort fast. Ask, “Why is our organization's performance declining?” and AI organizes an answer under headings such as leadership, motivation, and communication. The user can feel the relief of seeming to understand the problem before confirming any real cause, because a vague problem has become explainable.

But having an explanation is not the same as having found the cause. One can take the AI's classification as a starting point, or accept it as the conclusion. In the latter case, the feeling of having an answer stops questions that should still be asked. This can be understood from the closure perspective, which holds that people seize a conclusion quickly and keep it.

The core of this mechanism is not “I don't want to think” but “being in a state of still not knowing is uncomfortable.” The risk is not only that AI gives a wrong answer. It is also that the user feels reassured too soon and ends the exploration.

## 03 · Wanting Less Thinking Effort: Handing Cognitive Work to the Outside

The second mechanism concerns the effort needed to produce an answer more than the answer's certainty. Even when you know what to do, reading sources, comparing them, and building an argument yourself can be hard. Here, people turn to AI not to remove ambiguity but to reduce the burden of the work.

Risko and Gilbert (2016) described reducing cognitive demands by using tools or external actions as **cognitive offloading**. Writing down what to remember and leaving calculation to a calculator are typical examples. Using external tools is itself a natural cognitive strategy for humans.

Generative AI widens what can be offloaded. It goes beyond storing material or calculating, to summarizing, comparing, and composing arguments. A user can get the gist without reading a long report, or receive a recommendation without comparing each alternative.

The problem is that relieving effort and doing the thinking can happen together. Having AI tidy a document's format differs from having it decide which evidence matters. The former reduces repetitive work; the latter can mean handing over a process one's own judgment needs.

This difference matters in learning. In Bastani et al.'s (2025) high school mathematics study, doing well on practice problems with AI help did not carry over to better performance without AI. By contrast, a design that gave hints supporting reasoning rather than handing over the answer mitigated the negative learning effect. What mattered was whether AI produced the answer for learners or supported them in thinking.

The core of this mechanism is not “I dislike not knowing” but “I want to reduce the burden of thinking for myself.” The risk is that the work gets done while the ability to do it is not practiced enough.

## 04 · Wanting to Feel Competent: Taking a Good Output as One's Own Ability

The third mechanism concerns evaluation of oneself more than discomfort or effort. People want not only to finish the work but to feel they are someone who can do it well.

Ryan and Deci's (2000) self-determination theory proposes the **need for competence**, the wish to feel one acts effectively and exercises one's abilities, as one of the basic psychological needs.

Generative AI can provide this feeling quickly. Someone unsure of their writing can obtain a polished proposal, and can produce an analysis in a professional format even in an unfamiliar field. With a good output in front of them, they can feel relief that “the work is done” along with the achievement of “I can do this much too.”

Of course, this sense of achievement is not all illusion. Defining the problem, asking the right questions, and verifying and revising the answer are also the user's abilities. What must be distinguished is the ability to make an output together with AI and the ability to understand and judge for oneself what the output contains.

Suppose someone produced a market analysis report with AI. The report is excellent, but if the user cannot explain its key assumptions or examine counterevidence, there is a gap between the level of the output and the level of understanding. Taking the report's polish as one's own expertise then makes it hard to notice what more there is to learn.

A preprint on the relation between AI explanations and understanding also raised the possibility that interacting with a chatbot can make actual understanding and the feeling of having understood diverge (Aslanov et al., 2025). So confidence gained through AI should not be taken at once as evidence of understanding.

The core of this mechanism is not “I want to save effort” but “I want to feel like someone who is good at this.” The risk is confusing the experience of getting a good output with evidence that one's own skill has grown.

## 05 · When the Three Combine, AI Naturally Becomes What People Want

Applying the three mechanisms to one situation makes the differences clear. For someone using AI before writing a report, three wishes can operate together.

- “I don't know which direction to write in, so I want to decide quickly.” This is the first mechanism. AI eases the vagueness but may end exploration too early.
- “I know the direction, but I want to spare myself the work of reading sources and building the logic.” This is the second mechanism. AI lightens the load but may take over the chance to practice thinking.
- “I want to make a good report and feel that I am good at my work too.” This is the third mechanism. AI gives a sense of achievement but may blur the quality of the output with one's own competence.

Seen this way, AI dependence is not simply a matter of using a convenient tool often. It is a matter of how much we hand over to AI of tolerating uncertainty, thinking for ourselves, and assessing our own abilities accurately.

So after using AI, three different questions are needed: “Did I explore the problem enough?”, “Did I make the key judgment myself?”, and “Could I explain this result without AI?”

The relief AI gives is not the same as solving the problem, the effort AI saves is not the same as one's own learning, and a result made together with AI is not the same as independent competence. Understanding the appeal of generative AI begins with distinguishing these three differences.

## 06 · Designing AI So the Three Needs Become Learning

The three needs are not weaknesses to block but starting points for design. The same wish can lead AI to become a tool that thinks for us, or one that helps us think better. That Bastani et al. (2025) found an AI designed to give reasoning hints instead of answers mitigated the negative learning effect supports this direction. The concrete uses below are this piece's proposals.

| Need | If used as is | If designed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| A definite answer | Exploration ends at the first answer | AI structures exploration with questions instead of answers | Socratic questioning tutor |
| Less thinking effort | Judgment is handed over too | Split what to delegate from what to keep; give hints only when stuck | Person writes first, AI critiques and compares |
| Feeling competent | Output quality is mistaken for skill | Confirm understanding before giving the sense of achievement | Explain-then-feedback, variation problems |

**1. A definite answer: a Socratic questioning tutor.** The need for closure is the wish to reach a conclusion quickly. If AI, instead of answering at once, asks one at a time, “What assumptions does this judgment rest on?”, “Is there another explanation?”, and “What data has been confirmed so far?”, it keeps AI's strength of easing vagueness while exploration does not end. In the example of asking why organizational performance fell, AI would first ask which indicator fell and since when, before listing leadership or motivation. Another way is to ask it, for each candidate cause, to find evidence that could refute it.

**2. Less thinking effort: separate what to offload from what to do yourself.** Delegate repetitive work such as formatting, summarizing sources, and translation, and have people first write down the judgment of which evidence matters. Then have AI compare against it and push back. This is the same order as the Generative Cognitive Pause (GCP) discussed earlier (/weekly-insight/2026-10-11-ai-paradox-hrd/). In learning settings, a tutor that does not give the full solution at once but offers one hint at a time at the point of being stuck fits this approach.

**3. Feeling competent: give an achievement confirmed by understanding.** There is no need to block the sense of achievement a good output gives. But it can be made to rest on actual understanding. Before AI hands over the output, the user explains the key assumptions in their own words; after the explanation, AI gives feedback and a variation problem with changed conditions. Keeping a separate record of “what I can explain without AI” lets people see for themselves how a result made with AI differs from their independent competence.

None of the three is a proposal to reduce AI's convenience. At the moment AI fills what people want, the design adds one more step so that the need leads to learning.

### Insight Questions

1. Does the AI our organization uses give answers at once, or does it **ask back and widen exploration**?
2. Have we distinguished the work to hand to AI from the **judgments people make first**?
3. What mechanisms ensure the sense of competence members gain from AI leads to **understanding they can explain without AI**?
