### From Solving Given Problems to Creating Problems Worth Solving

Luong, T., & Lockhart, E. (2025, July 21). Advanced version of Gemini with Deep Think officially achieves gold-medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad. *Google DeepMind*.

This is the author's own perspective piece. It sorts the problems organizations handle by time direction into fix-it, exploratory, and creative types, starts from a case in which AI extends to high-level reasoning (the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad), and asks where human value moves.

The sorting of problem types and the claim about connecting experience, reflection, and imagination are the author's proposals. The Olympiad case concerns mathematics, which differs from organizational problems entangled with interests and accountability, as the essay itself notes.
</div>

## 01 · Why Organizations Have Needed Problem Solvers

An organization can be seen as a group that gathers to handle problems of a scale and complexity that an individual can hardly solve alone. People with different knowledge and experience divide roles and cooperate to produce results that individual effort could not. From this view, an organization's reason for existing lies in solving problems and creating value through those solutions.

So organizations have long needed people who are good at solving problems. Those who analyze causes, compare alternatives, and find workable solutions were judged capable talent.

But in an era when AI rapidly extends problem-solving ability, is that standard still enough? In the future, can we explain our value merely by solving given problems faster and more accurately than others?

The importance of problem solving will not disappear. It is not enough by itself, however. What will matter more is the ability to create problems that have not yet been given, and to add thought to them until they become challenges worth taking on together.

## 02 · Problems That Restore the Past, Improve the Present, and Create the Future

The problems organizations handle can be sorted into three types by the direction of time.

The first faces the past: **"fix-it problems."** A defect has occurred, a customer has left, a project is delayed. We must diagnose what went wrong and restore a normal state, like putting out a fire in a burning house. A problem that has already occurred is the starting point for action.

The second seeks to change the present: **"exploratory problems."** Nothing is broken right now, but we look for a better way: raising productivity with the same resources, improving customer experience, making work procedures more efficient. The aim is to narrow the gap between current practice and a better one.

In both, what should be solved or improved is already given to some degree, so the ability to analyze causes and find suitable solutions matters.

The third faces the future: **"creative problems."** No problem has occurred yet, and no one has asked for improvement. Yet new questions arise. "Why must it be the way it is now?" "If this thing that does not yet exist became possible, what would change?"

Here, before solving a problem, we must first create what to treat as a problem. A new problem emerges not from a present defect but between an imagined possibility and reality.

## 03 · The Better AI Answers, the More It Matters What We Pursue

AI's progress makes us rethink the human role in the first two areas. At the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad, an advanced Gemini Deep Think model solved five of the six problems perfectly, a performance at the gold-medal standard (Luong & Lockhart, 2025). This does not mean every hard problem in mathematics is solved, but it shows that AI's abilities are extending even into high-level reasoning.

Of course, organizational problems differ from mathematics problems. Interests and conflicts of values exist, and execution and accountability must be considered amid incomplete information. We cannot assume that AI is better than humans at every problem.

Even so, if a large part of analysis and the search for alternatives can be done together with AI, where should humans create greater value?

I believe the center should expand from the ability to find given answers to the ability to create problems worth pursuing: beyond asking what we can do, judging what is worth trying. The richer solutions become, the more important it is to decide which problems deserve our time and resources.

## 04 · How Was the Problem of Mars Created?

Imagine a future in which people live on Mars. This challenge is not explained as answering a demand or complaint such as "Earth is too uncomfortable to live on, so find a way to move us to Mars."

That does not mean Earth has no problems. It means that, when we look at the challenge of Mars, no problem that could only be solved by going to Mars occurred first, and no demand for improvement was given first.

Yet a problem was created, through the question "Can humans live on another planet?"

The problem was not that a problem arose and a solution was sought. By imagining a new possibility, people created the problems that must be solved to reach it. Once we begin to ask how to build an environment people can live in, how to secure water and energy, and how to endure long travel and isolation, imagination becomes concrete tasks.

What matters here is not the unusual idea itself. It is the process of developing that idea into a problem others can think about and explore together. A single question about the future connects different kinds of expertise and creates a reason to cooperate.

An organization's reason for existing can expand from here, too. It can go beyond a group that gathers to solve given problems and become one that creates problems worth pursuing together and realizes their possibilities.

## 05 · New Problems Grow from Experience and Reflection

This does not mean that clever ideas will matter more than expertise in the future. Imagination detached from experience and knowledge easily stays a slogan. The ability to create new problems starts from a deep understanding of reality.

People who meet customers in the field, go through trial and error, and experience the gap between plan and reality develop questions of their own. Even looking at the same material, people come up with different questions because what each has experienced and what each values differ.

Reflection must be added. We must ask why this approach worked, what we took for granted, and what possibilities we missed. When we look back at our own judgment rather than merely accumulating experience, solving problems in the past can lead to questions about the future.

Imagination extends those questions: going beyond "let's do better next time" to ask "could there have been a different way in the first place?" It is the ability to look again at familiar constraints, connect different experiences, and picture possibilities that do not yet exist.

The competency the future needs lies in connecting experience, reflection, and imagination: discovering a sense of problem from one's own experience, examining existing assumptions through reflection, and developing them into new challenges through imagination.

## 06 · People Who Create Problems and Add Thought to Them

Coming up with a new question differs from developing it. We must keep thinking about why this problem should be addressed, who it is valuable to and how, and what to check first.

"Creating a problem" does not mean adding unnecessary work. It means looking anew at what we had not treated as a problem and defining it as a challenge worth taking on. It turns the imagination "might this be possible too?" into the inquiry "then what must we know, and what should we test first?"

AI can be a partner in this process too. There is no need to divide the work so that only humans create problems and only AI solves them. What matters is to keep our own view of what we pursue and why it matters, even while exploring possibilities with AI.

So organizations and HRD should not evaluate only how quickly set problems were solved. They should look at what questions members found in the field and how they developed them into meaningful tasks. They should offer chances to propose one's own questions, review them with colleagues, and refine them through small experiments. If every question whose usefulness is not yet clear is treated as inefficiency, education that stresses imagination alone will struggle to change anything.

In the future, too, organizations will be groups that solve problems. But those problems will not all come from outside. Questions arising from members' experience, reflection, and imagination can become an organization's next reason to exist.

So the person the future needs must not remain someone who brings back answers quickly. That person must create problems, add thought to them, and develop them into challenges worth exploring with others.

If the ability to solve given problems well produces today's results, the ability to create problems worth solving together builds an organization's next future.

---

### Insight Questions

1. Does our organization have **a place** where questions members discover in the field can be proposed and refined?
2. Are we evaluating only **how quickly** set problems were solved?
3. Are we helping people keep **their own view of what to pursue** while they explore possibilities with AI?
